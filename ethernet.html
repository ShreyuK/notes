What Ethernet actually is

Ethernet is a family of wired networking standards maintained by the IEEE 802.3 group. The trick that kept it alive for decades: the top half (the frame format) never changed, while the bottom half (speed, cable, voltages) was reinvented again and again. TI's article puts it this way: IEEE kept adding new data rates, new physical media and new PHY functions, so Ethernet grew far beyond office computers.

Compared with the buses you know (UART, SPI, I2C), Ethernet is built for tens to hundreds of metres, many nodes, and electrically hostile places like factories and cars.

Mental model. Think of the post office. The MAC writes the envelope (addresses, checksum). The PHY is the truck driver: it knows nothing about the letter, only how to move it over the road. A switch is the sorting centre that reads the address and picks the right road.

Where Ethernet sits in the OSI model

The OSI model splits networking into seven layers. Ethernet lives in the bottom two: layer 1 (Physical) and layer 2 (Data Link). Everything above, like IP and TCP, rides inside Ethernet frames. Tap a layer below.

Layer 2 is really two jobs

The MAC sublayer builds frames, checks the CRC, and (in half-duplex) manages collisions. The Logical Link sublayer on top identifies the protocol. In hardware, the MAC is digital logic inside your MCU, SoC, FPGA or switch chip. Layer 1 is the PHY, the mixed-signal chip that touches the cable.

MAC and PHY: the two halves of a port

Digital domain. The PHY talks to the MAC through a Media Independent Interface, or one of its variants (next sections). "Media independent" means the MAC does not care what cable or speed is behind the PHY. Analog domain. On the cable side sits the Medium Dependent Interface (MDI), a continuous, time-varying signal. This is where 10BASE-T, 100BASE-TX, 1000BASE-T and the T1 family all differ.

Inside the PHY: on transmit, scrambling, line coding, pulse shaping and a line driver; on receive, an analog front end, equaliser, clock recovery PLL, decoder. Plus auto-negotiation, link LEDs and a management register block.

What happens on the wire

Differential pairs

CAT5/5e/6 cable holds four twisted pairs. Each signal is sent as +V on one wire and -V on its partner; the receiver reads only the difference. Interference hits both wires equally and cancels, and twisting keeps that coupling equal. Nominal differential impedance is 100 ohms, so traces and terminations must match to avoid reflections.

Magnetics: why there is a transformer

Between PHY and connector, BASE-T ports use a magnetics module that provides galvanic isolation (typically about 1.5 kV), common-mode noise rejection through chokes, and coupling between driver and cable. This is a big part of the "external components" cost the article lists against 100BASE-TX. Single-pair automotive PHYs usually get by with AC-coupling capacitors and a common-mode choke.

Auto-negotiation

Plug in a cable and each PHY fires Fast Link Pulses carrying a 16-bit word listing what it supports (speed, duplex, flow control). Both sides read the other's word and pick the best common mode by a priority table: 1000 full beats 100 full beats 10. Classic bug: force one side to a fixed speed while the other auto-negotiates and you get a duplex mismatch, which means slow links and CRC errors. Single-pair automotive PHYs generally use master/slave configuration instead.

Heads-up on the article's Table 2: it lists 802.3u for 10BASE-T/Te and 802.3bu for 100BASE-T1. As far as I know, those are 802.3i / 802.3az and 802.3bw. Verify in the standard before citing.

Switches: hubs, unmanaged, managed

Everything so far was one link. A network needs many nodes, and that is the switch's job. Three generations to know:

Hub (layer 1). Repeats every bit to every port. One shared collision domain, half-duplex, everyone hears everything. Obsolete. Switch (layer 2). Reads the destination MAC address and forwards the frame only to the right port, so each port is its own collision domain and full-duplex is normal.

Managed features in plain words

VLAN: carve one physical switch into separate logical networks using a 4-byte 802.1Q tag in the frame. QoS: priority queues so a control message beats a bulk download. Spanning tree: disables redundant links until needed so loops never form. Port mirroring: copies traffic to a monitor port for debugging. IGMP snooping: stops multicast from flooding every port. Link aggregation: bundles ports for bandwidth.

Choosing a PHY: the article's process

Work down three specs that vendors publish. 1. Data rate: pick 10, 100 or 1000 Mbps from the application. 2. Standard: consumer and most industrial gear uses 10BASE-Te, 100BASE-TX and 1000BASE-T because PCs speak them; automotive uses BASE-T1, which is completely different from BASE-T. Exception: automotive OBD ports normally use BASE-T/TX so a PC can plug in. 3. MAC interface: make sure your MCU or SoC and the PHY share an interface (RMII for small MCUs, RGMII for application processors).

Beyond the article, also weigh latency, power and heat, temperature grade (industrial or AEC-Q100), EMC budget, I/O voltage, clocking, package, and features such as cable diagnostics, IEEE 1588 timestamping and Energy Efficient Ethernet. TI's article names the DP83826E (low-latency 10/100 for Industry 4.0 and daisy chains) and the DP83TC811S-Q1 (100BASE-T1 automotive). Its selection flowchart is an image in the PDF; open the original to see it. Part 2 of the series covers schematic and layout practice.

Schematic checklist

Clock. A 25 MHz crystal or oscillator feeds the PHY (RMII systems often share one 50 MHz source with the MAC). Aim for tight tolerance, roughly 50 ppm in practice (the standard allows about 100 ppm), and choose load capacitors from the PHY datasheet and crystal CL. Poor frequency accuracy shows up as random CRC errors and failed links to other vendors' gear.

Power. PHYs often need several rails: an analog supply (for example 3.3 V), a core rail (around 1.0 to 1.2 V, sometimes made internally), and an I/O rail matching your MAC (1.8 to 3.3 V). Decouple each pin group, filter analog rails with ferrite beads, and check the sequencing requirements. Gigabit PHYs run warm, so check package thermal pad and copper area.

Reset and straps. Hold reset low until supplies and clock are stable, then wait the datasheet time before touching MDIO. At reset release the PHY samples strapping pins to set its MDIO address, interface mode, auto-negotiation defaults and RGMII delay. Use the recommended pull-up/pull-down values (commonly a few kilohms) and watch for LED pins that double as straps: an LED wired the wrong way can flip the strap.

Magnetics and termination. Pick a module rated for your speed, with isolation voltage that meets your safety standard. The PHY-side center taps are either tied to a supply or left to capacitors depending on whether the driver is current-mode or voltage-mode. On the cable side, unused pairs are commonly terminated with 75 ohm resistors to a high-voltage capacitor to chassis (called Bob Smith termination), which damps common-mode noise and reflections.

Protection. The RJ45 is an antenna and a lightning rod. Add low-capacitance TVS arrays suited to the data rate where your ESD or surge target requires it, and keep them close to the connector. Integrated-magnetics RJ45 jacks save board area and often simplify layout.

Layout rules that matter

Differential routing. Keep pairs at 100 ohm differential impedance, tightly length-matched within a pair, short, and with as few vias and layer changes as possible. Keep other signals and the crystal away from them.

Magnetics area. Keep the PHY side and the cable side separated, with no copper plane underneath the cable-side pins, so the isolation barrier stays intact and noise can't couple across. Connect the connector shield to a chassis-ground region, and tie chassis to signal ground in one deliberate place (often through a capacitor or resistor in parallel with a high-voltage cap, per the design's EMC strategy).

RGMII. Match data lines to their clock within the datasheet's skew window and let the PHY's internal delay (commonly 2 ns) provide the clock offset, rather than meandering traces. RMII/MII. Source-terminate if the traces are long, and keep clocks away from the analog side.

Bring-up and debugging

Power up in this order: check rails and clock with a scope, release reset, then read the PHY identifier registers over MDIO (registers 2 and 3 in IEEE 802.3 clause 22 PHYs). If those read garbage, suspect address straps, MDIO pull-up (it needs one), or clock. Register 1 holds the link-status bit; register 0 controls reset, speed, auto-negotiation and loopback; registers 4 and 5 hold your advertised abilities and the partner's. Use PHY loopback modes to separate MAC, PHY and cable faults. Then look at link LEDs, per-port error counters and (where the PHY has it) built-in cable diagnostics, which work like a TDR to find opens, shorts and the distance to them.

Cable and signal integrity numbers

CAT5e is specified to 100 MHz, CAT6 to 250 MHz, CAT6A to 500 MHz. The main impairments are insertion loss (the signal weakens with length, hence the 100 m limit for BASE-T), return loss (reflections from impedance mismatch), and near-end crosstalk, called NEXT (a pair's own transmit leaking into its neighbours). Gigabit's echo canceller and equaliser exist to fight exactly these effects.

Power over Ethernet in two lines

PoE (IEEE 802.3af/at/bt) sends DC power alongside data, tapped through the magnetics' center taps or over spare pairs, so a PoE port adds a PD controller, a rectifier bridge and an isolated DC-DC converter to your hardware. 10BASE-T1L and automotive links use PoDL instead, which couples power onto the single data pair.

Glossary, explained

Auto-negotiation
PHYs swap capability words using fast link pulses and agree on the best common speed and duplex.
Auto MDI-X
The PHY swaps its TX and RX pairs automatically, so cable type stops mattering.
BASE
Baseband signalling: the signal occupies the cable directly, no radio-style carrier.
Bob Smith termination
75 ohm resistors on unused or center-tap pairs tied through a capacitor to chassis, to damp common-mode noise.
CAT5e / CAT6 / CAT6A
Twisted-pair cable grades with tested bandwidth of 100, 250 and 500 MHz.
Collision domain
The set of devices that can interfere with each other's transmissions. A switch gives each port its own.
Common-mode choke
Coupled inductor that blocks noise appearing equally on both wires while passing the differential signal.
CRC / FCS
The 32-bit checksum at the end of every frame. A bad FCS means the frame is discarded.
CRS / COL
MII signals: carrier sense (line is busy) and collision detect (half-duplex only).
DDR
Double data rate: data is transferred on both rising and falling clock edges, as in RGMII.
Duplex
Full duplex sends and receives at once; half duplex takes turns and needs collision handling.
EEE
Energy Efficient Ethernet (802.3az): the link sleeps between bursts to save power.
EMC / EMI
Electromagnetic compatibility is your product coping with noise; interference is the noise itself, including what you emit.
FIFO
First-in-first-out buffer; RMII receive uses one to bridge clock differences, which adds latency variation.
FLP
Fast link pulse: a burst of pulses carrying a 16-bit capability word during auto-negotiation.
Frame
The layer-2 packet: addresses, type, payload and FCS.
Galvanic isolation
No direct electrical path between two sides; signals cross through a transformer, protecting against ground differences.
IGMP snooping
Managed-switch feature that sends multicast only to ports that asked for it.
Line coding
The rule for turning bits into voltage levels: Manchester, MLT-3, PAM-3, PAM-5.
MAC address
The 48-bit hardware address of an interface, used for layer-2 delivery.
MDI / MDIO / MDC
MDI is the analog cable-side interface; MDIO and MDC are the two-wire data and clock lines used to read and write PHY registers.
Magnetics
The transformer and choke module between PHY and connector.
Manchester
10BASE-T coding with a transition in every bit period, carrying clock and data together.
MLT-3
Three-level code (+1, 0, -1) that cycles slowly to cut the signal's frequency in 100BASE-TX.
NEXT
Near-end crosstalk: your transmit pair leaking into a neighbouring pair at the same end.
OBD
On-board diagnostics: the car's service port, which often uses BASE-T/TX for PC access.
PAM-3 / PAM-5
Pulse amplitude modulation with three or five voltage levels per symbol.
PLC
Programmable logic controller, an industrial automation computer.
PoE / PoDL
Power over Ethernet on 4-pair cable, and power over data lines on a single pair.
QoS
Quality of service: priority queues so urgent traffic is forwarded first.
Return loss
How much signal reflects back from impedance mismatches; higher return loss (in dB) means fewer reflections.
STP (cable) / STP (protocol)
Shielded twisted pair is a cable; spanning tree protocol (and RSTP) blocks redundant switch links to prevent loops. Context tells which.
Strapping pins
Pins sampled at reset to set the PHY's default configuration.
TVS
Transient voltage suppressor, a clamp diode for ESD and surge.
UTP
Unshielded twisted pair.
VLAN
Virtual LAN: logical network separation using an 802.1Q tag in the frame.
