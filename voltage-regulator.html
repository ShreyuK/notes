Linear Regulators

A controlled variable resistor in series with the load — it burns the difference between VIN and VOUT as heat.

How it actually works

A linear regulator places a series pass element (a BJT or MOSFET) between the input rail and the output. This transistor behaves like a voltage-controlled resistor: it is never fully on or fully off, it sits in its active/linear region, continuously adjusting its channel resistance so that the drop across it plus VOUT always equals VIN.

An error amplifier is the brain of the loop. It compares a scaled-down copy of VOUT (via a resistor divider R1/R2, or an internal trim for fixed parts) against an internal bandgap reference VREF (typically 1.2 V or 0.6–1.25 V depending on IC). Any error between the two drives the gate/base of the pass transistor, closing a negative-feedback loop that forces the divider node to sit exactly at VREF.

Because the divider node is forced to VREF, the output voltage is set purely by the resistor ratio:

VOUT = VREF × (1 + R1/R2)

For fixed-voltage parts (78xx, most LDOs) R1/R2 are built in on-chip. For adjustable parts (LM317, AMS1117-ADJ) you set R1/R2 externally to program any output above VREF.

Key datasheet parameters

  • Dropout voltage (VDO): minimum (VIN − VOUT) needed to keep the pass transistor in its linear region. Classic 78xx ≈ 2 V; a true LDO ("Low DropOut") is 0.1–0.5 V, achieved by using a PMOS/PNP pass element instead of an NPN follower.
  • Quiescent current (IQ): current the IC itself consumes (bias + error amp), not delivered to the load. Ranges from single-digit µA (battery-friendly LDOs) to a few mA. Matters a lot in always-on / battery circuits.
  • PSRR (Power Supply Rejection Ratio): how well the regulator rejects ripple/noise present on VIN from reaching VOUT, in dB vs frequency. This is the single biggest reason to choose linear over switching for sensitive analog/RF rails.
  • Load regulation: how much VOUT sags as IOUT increases from no-load to full-load, at constant VIN.
  • Line regulation: how much VOUT shifts as VIN changes, at constant IOUT.
  • Output noise (µVRMS): broadband noise from the reference and error amp, important for RF/ADC supplies — often reduced with an external "NR/NC" bypass capacitor.
  • Thermal resistance θJA/θJC: since all excess power becomes heat inside the package, this sets your real maximum current at a given ΔV and ambient temperature.
  • Stability requirements: many LDOs need a specific output capacitor range and minimum ESR to stay stable — check the datasheet's stable region, not just "any capacitor."

Where linear regulators belong

Use caseWhy linear wins here
Clean analog / RF suppliesNo switching noise at all; high PSRR kills input ripple — ideal for ADCs, PLLs, VCOs, sensitive sensors.
Post-regulator after a buckBuck gets you close and efficient (e.g. 5→3.6 V), LDO cleans the last stretch to 3.3 V — best of both worlds.
Battery / always-on railsµA-quiescent LDOs sipping power on standby circuits; small ΔV keeps efficiency acceptable even though it's linear.
Simple, cheap suppliesThree terminals, two capacitors, done — no inductor, no switching-loop layout to fight.
Adjustable bench supplyLM317-style: VOUT = 1.25 × (1 + R2/R1), a classic building block.

Efficiency & heat — the two graphs, explained

Efficiency (%) vs V_IN — V_OUT = 3.3 V, I_OUT = 1 A
Heat Dissipated (W) vs V_IN — same conditions
Why the efficiency curve falls the way it does: for a linear regulator, efficiency is simply η = VOUT/VIN (input power ≈ VIN×IOUT, output power = VOUT×IOUT, same current flows through both). That's a pure hyperbola (η ∝ 1/VIN) — it falls fast at first and flattens out at high VIN, exactly the concave shape in the plot. At 3.6 V in it's 92% efficient (barely any headroom to burn); by 12 V in it's only 28%, because 72% of the input power is being thrown away as heat for no benefit.
Why the heat curve rises the way it does: Pdiss = (VIN − VOUT) × IOUT. With IOUT fixed at 1 A, this is a straight line in (VIN − VOUT) — which is exactly what the plot shows: linear growth from ~0 W near dropout to 8.7 W at 12 V in. This is the practical design consequence: every extra volt of headroom above what you need costs you a fixed extra watt per amp of load, and that watt has to leave the package through the PCB copper or a heatsink, or the thermal shutdown will trip.

When NOT to use a linear regulator

  • Large (VIN − VOUT) at high current — the heat budget becomes impossible without a serious heatsink or forced airflow.
  • Step-up is physically impossible — a linear regulator can only ever regulate down; VOUT must always be less than VIN minus dropout.
  • Battery-life-critical designs with a wide VIN range (e.g. Li-ion 4.2 V → 3.3 V logic across full discharge) — efficiency swings and wastes capacity.

Switching Regulators — PWM Fundamentals

The pass device is switched fully ON or fully OFF, so it dissipates almost nothing itself — energy is stored and released through an inductor instead of being burned.

How it works

Instead of a transistor sitting in its lossy linear region, a switching regulator drives it as a hard switch: either fully saturated (≈0 V drop, so P = V×I ≈ 0) or fully open (≈0 A, so again P ≈ 0). Almost no power is dissipated in the switch itself — the only real losses are the small non-idealities (RDS(on), switching transition times, diode drop, inductor DCR, capacitor ESR).

Energy delivered to the output is chopped into pulses by the switch, then stored temporarily in an inductor's magnetic field and smoothed by an output capacitor, so the load sees a continuous, roughly-DC voltage even though the switch node itself is a square wave.

A PWM controller senses VOUT through a feedback path and adjusts the duty cycle D = tON/T (the fraction of each switching period the switch spends ON) to hold the output at the target voltage regardless of load or input changes.

Core concepts

  • Duty cycle D = tON/T is the primary control variable — it directly sets the DC output level.
  • Switching frequency typically ranges 100 kHz to 2 MHz+. Higher frequency → smaller L and C (smaller solution size) but higher switching losses.
  • Topologies: buck (step-down), boost (step-up), buck-boost/SEPIC (either direction), flyback (isolated, for mains-referenced designs).
  • An ideal switch + ideal L and C would lose nothing; real converters still reach 85–95%+ efficiency because the loss mechanisms are proportional to I²R and switching transition energy, not to the full ΔV like a linear regulator.
  • Trade-offs versus linear: switching noise and EMI at the switching frequency and its harmonics, output ripple, more external parts, and a layout where loop area and ground routing are critical, not optional.

The two characteristic graphs

Efficiency (%) vs Load Current (A)
Why it dips at light load and droops at high load: at very low load current, fixed switching losses (gate drive charge × frequency, controller quiescent current) dominate a small output power, so efficiency collapses. As load rises, output power grows faster than these fixed losses, so efficiency climbs — the curve is essentially flat and high (90%+) across the middle of the range. At high current, I²R conduction losses (switch RDS(on), inductor DCR, trace resistance) start to dominate again and efficiency droops slightly. The result is the characteristic rounded peak / plateau shape, very different from the linear regulator's monotonic hyperbola.

PWM output relationship (Buck, ideal, continuous conduction)

Buck Average Output (V) vs Duty Cycle (%) — V_IN = 12 V
The switch node toggles between VIN and 0 V; the LC filter simply averages that square wave. The time-average of a square wave switching between 0 and VIN with duty cycle D is exactly D × VIN — a straight line through the origin with slope VIN, which is exactly what the plot shows. This is why buck-derived control is so linear and predictable: VOUT = D × VIN, no exponentials, no surprises (that changes for boost — see Section 4).

Buck Converter

Step-down switching — VOUT = D × VIN

Switching cycle, phase by phase

Switch ON (energizing)

The high-side switch closes, connecting VIN to the switch node. The full (VIN − VOUT) appears across the inductor, so inductor current ramps up (dI/dt = VL/L). Energy is being pushed into both the inductor's field and the load simultaneously.

Switch OFF (freewheeling)

The high-side switch opens. Inductor current cannot change instantly, so it continues flowing in the same direction, now pulled from ground through a diode (async) or a low-side FET (sync) — this is the "freewheeling" path. The inductor voltage flips sign, now equal to −VOUT, so current ramps back down until the next cycle.

Steady state (CCM): VOUT = D × VIN   |   D = VOUT/VIN

Synchronous vs Asynchronous — why the efficiency gap

Efficiency (%) vs Load Current (A) — 12 V → 3.3 V example
Why synchronous wins: in the async design the freewheeling diode has a roughly fixed forward drop (~0.4 V for a good Schottky) regardless of current. On a 3.3 V rail that's ~12% of the output lost to the diode alone during every freewheel phase. Replacing the diode with a low-side MOSFET (sync rectification) drops that to I×RDS(on), which can be a few mV — and shrinks further as you choose a lower RDS(on) FET.
When async is still fine: at higher output voltages (12 V+) the fixed diode drop is a small fraction of VOUT, so the efficiency penalty shrinks. It's also the simpler, cheaper choice for cost-sensitive, low-current designs, and it has no light-load reverse-current issue the way sync converters can (current can flow backward through a low-side FET at very light load unless the controller adds diode-emulation mode).
04

Boost Converter

Step-up switching — VOUT = VIN / (1 − D)

Switching cycle, phase by phase

Switch ON (charging L)

The low-side switch closes to ground. The full VIN now appears directly across the inductor, so its current ramps up, storing energy in the magnetic field. The diode is reverse-biased during this phase (VOUT > the switch-node voltage), so it blocks — the output capacitor alone supplies the load during this time.

Switch OFF (boosting)

The switch opens. Inductor current must keep flowing, so its voltage flips polarity and adds on top of VIN. This combined voltage (VIN + VL) forward-biases the diode and pushes current into COUT and the load, so VOUT ends up higher than VIN.

Steady state (CCM): VOUT = VIN / (1 − D)
No switch in the input path: notice there is no series switch between VIN and the output — a shorted output is fed directly from VIN through L and D with nothing to interrupt it. Always add input/output fusing or current limiting; the topology itself provides none.

Output voltage vs duty cycle — why it explodes near D = 1

Output Voltage (V) vs Duty Cycle (%) — V_IN = 5 V
VOUT = VIN/(1−D) is a rectangular-hyperbola with a vertical asymptote at D = 1: as D approaches 1, (1−D) approaches 0 and the theoretical gain goes to infinity. In practice you never get there — parasitic resistances (switch RDS(on), inductor DCR, diode drop) eat an increasing share of the transferred energy as D climbs, so real designs plateau and then fall beyond roughly D ≈ 0.85. That's why the curve in the plot bends upward increasingly steeply rather than actually blowing up — the practical, loss-limited region is D up to about 0.7–0.85 depending on the design.

Typical applications

  • Single Li-ion cell (3.7 V) → 5 V USB or 12 V rail
  • LED backlight and LED-string drivers
  • PFC front-end stage in mains-referenced supplies
  • Energy harvesting from millivolt-level input sources

Design cautions

  • Input current exceeds output current: IIN = IOUT/(1−D) — size input traces/inductor accordingly.
  • The diode and switch both see the full VOUT — rate them for it plus margin.
  • A right-half-plane (RHP) zero in the control-to-output transfer function limits achievable loop bandwidth — boost loops are inherently slower/harder to compensate than buck.
  • Cannot regulate VOUT below VIN − VF (diode always provides a DC path from input to output).

Linear vs Switching — Choosing the Right Regulator

Every property trades off against the others; the right choice is set by the specific constraint that matters most in your design.

PropertyLinear RegulatorSwitching Regulator
Efficiencyη = VOUT/VIN — 30–90%, often poor85–95%+ nearly always
HeatP = (VIN−VOUT)·I — can be largeSmall, often no heatsink needed
Noise / rippleEssentially none, high PSRRSwitching ripple + EMI at fSW and harmonics
Step up?Never — down onlyBuck, boost, invert — all possible
Parts countIC + 2 capacitorsIC + inductor + capacitor(s) + diode/FET
Size at powerBig heatsink needed at high powerStays compact even at high power
CostVery lowHigher (inductor, more complex IC)
PCB layoutForgivingCritical — switch-node loop area, ground routing
Transient responseFast, simple single-pole loopSlower, needs loop compensation
Best forClean analog, small ΔV, low powerEfficiency-critical, battery, high power

The decisive graph

Efficiency (%) vs V_IN — delivering 3.3 V at 1 A
The buck stays flat near 90%+ across the whole input range because its losses scale with the small non-idealities of the switch and inductor, not with ΔV. The linear regulator's efficiency collapses linearly toward zero as VIN rises, because it is bound to η = VOUT/VIN — the wider the gap, the more of the input power is simply thrown away as heat. This single plot is the entire argument for switching regulators in any design with a meaningfully wide VIN–VOUT gap.

Choose LINEAR when…

  • Noise matters (analog front-ends, RF, ADC references)
  • VIN − VOUT is small (LDO territory)
  • Current is low (well under a few hundred mA)
  • Simplicity or cost is the dominant constraint
  • Used as a post-regulator right after a buck stage

Choose SWITCHING when…

  • Efficiency matters (battery-powered systems)
  • Large VIN − VOUT gap
  • High current, where the heat budget would be impossible linearly
  • You must step voltage up, or invert its polarity
  • Space and thermal margin at the board level are tight

Key takeaways

  • Linear: the pass transistor burns (VIN−VOUT)·I as heat. Quiet and simple; efficiency = VOUT/VIN. LDO just means low dropout.
  • Switching: switch fully ON/OFF + inductor/capacitor energy storage → 85–95% efficiency. Costs you ripple, EMI, and layout discipline.
  • Buck: step-down, VOUT = D·VIN. A synchronous buck swaps the diode for a FET — roughly 10× lower freewheel-path loss.
  • Boost: step-up, VOUT = VIN/(1−D). The inductor's flyback voltage adds onto VIN. No inherent output-short protection — fuse it.
  • Choosing: big ΔV or high current → go switching. Noise-critical or a tiny ΔV → go linear. Many real designs combine both: buck stage followed by an LDO post-regulator.

Component Selection — What To Check Before You Pick a Part

Datasheet parameters that actually drive a working design, organized by what you're selecting.

Choosing the regulator IC (linear or switching)

Electrical fit

  • VIN range: must cover your worst-case min and max input, including transients (e.g. cold-crank, load dump in automotive).
  • VOUT — fixed or adjustable, and its tolerance/accuracy over line, load and temperature.
  • IOUT(max) with margin — don't run a part at its absolute datasheet limit continuously.
  • Dropout (linear) or minimum VIN−VOUT headroom (switching) at your worst-case battery-discharge or brown-out condition.
  • Efficiency curve at your actual operating current — check the datasheet plot at your load point, not just the headline peak number.

System-level fit

  • Protection features: over-current, over-temperature, UVLO (under-voltage lockout), and — for boost — whether it has any output overvoltage/short protection at all.
  • Soft-start: controlled ramp-up limits inrush current into downstream capacitance; check if built-in or needs external components.
  • Switching frequency (switchers): higher fSW shrinks L/C but raises switching loss and EMI; check against any EMI-sensitive bands you must avoid (AM radio, GPS).
  • Package & thermal resistance (θJA): determines your real continuous current rating on your actual PCB, not the datasheet's idealized test board.
  • Stability requirements: LDOs may need a specific COUT ESR range; switchers need loop compensation appropriate to your L/C selection — check the recommended external component table.
  • Cost and second-source availability for production designs.

Selecting the inductor (buck/boost)

  • Inductance value (L): sets ripple current — typically chosen so ripple is 20–40% of IOUT; too small = high ripple and core/EMI issues, too large = poor transient response and bigger physical size.
  • Saturation current (ISAT): must exceed your peak inductor current (IOUT + half the ripple, plus margin for transients/short-circuit) — saturating the core collapses inductance and can destroy the switch.
  • RMS/thermal current rating: where the winding itself heats up from I²R — check this is above your continuous operating current, separate from ISAT.
  • DC resistance (DCR): directly sets conduction loss (I²×DCR) — lower is more efficient but usually means a physically larger part.
  • Core material / shielding: shielded inductors reduce radiated EMI, important near sensitive analog circuitry.
  • Self-resonant frequency: should sit well above your switching frequency and its relevant harmonics.

Selecting the diode / synchronous FET

  • Forward voltage VF (diode): directly subtracts from efficiency, worse at low VOUT — prefer Schottky (~0.3–0.5 V) over standard silicon (~0.7 V).
  • Reverse voltage rating: must exceed the highest voltage it will block (VIN for buck freewheel diode, VOUT for boost diode) with margin for ringing.
  • Reverse recovery time (tRR): matters at higher switching frequencies — slow recovery diodes create shoot-through-like losses and spikes.
  • Synchronous FET RDS(on): sets conduction loss directly (I²×RDS(on)) — the main lever for pushing efficiency above what a diode-based design can reach.
  • Gate charge (QG): for FETs, higher QG costs more drive/switching loss at high frequency — a classic RDS(on) vs QG trade-off.
  • Body-diode characteristics of the sync FET still matter — it conducts briefly during the dead-time between switch transitions.

Selecting input/output capacitors

  • Capacitance value: input cap supplies the pulsed switch current locally; output cap smooths ripple and supports load transients — both sized from the controller's recommended range.
  • Voltage rating: keep meaningful margin above the maximum DC voltage seen (ceramics also lose real capacitance as DC bias approaches the rating — derate accordingly).
  • ESR (Equivalent Series Resistance): directly sets output ripple voltage (ΔV ≈ ΔI×ESR at the switching edges) and can also set/break loop stability on some LDOs — check the datasheet's required range.
  • Ripple current rating: especially critical on the input capacitor of a buck (sees the full pulsed switch current) — an undersized cap here overheats and fails early.
  • Dielectric type: X5R/X7R ceramics for low ESR and small size; electrolytic/polymer where higher capacitance or higher ripple-current handling is needed.

Feedback network & layout

  • Resistor tolerance (R1/R2 divider): directly sets output voltage accuracy — 1% resistors are the practical default for anything precision-sensitive.
  • Feedback trace routing: keep the feedback sense line away from the noisy switch node; on switchers, sense as close to the load as practical (remote/Kelvin sensing) for accurate load regulation.
  • Switch-node loop area: minimize the physical loop between switch, inductor, and input/output capacitors — this is the #1 EMI lever in a switching design.
  • Ground plane/star grounding: separate noisy power-stage return current from sensitive analog/feedback returns.
  • Thermal vias / copper pour: for both linear (heat) and switching (FET/diode conduction loss) designs, adequate copper area is what turns a datasheet current rating into a real one.
