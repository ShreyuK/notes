B1 — power architecture

The power tree, not just "add caps"

Power pins split into distinct domains — VDDAT (analog PHY, 3.3V), VDDIO (I/O ring, selectable 1.8/2.5/3.3V), and the internal core rail. The real topology bridges them deliberately, not just wires them together:

Why bulk caps on BOTH sides of the ferrite bead?
The bead blocks high-frequency noise from crossing between domains — that's its job. But if only one side has bulk capacitance, a current transient on the "empty" side has to pull current through the bead from the other domain, which is exactly the coupling path the bead is meant to prevent. Giving each domain its own local reservoir keeps that transient current local.

GND — isolation strategy

Signal ground vs. chassis ground

The transformer's whole purpose is broken if the two grounds are tied solidly together right at the chip. They're kept apart and bridged in exactly one place, far from the switch IC:

Common mistake Transformer center taps (both TX and RX, chip side) must connect only to signal ground through a 0.1–10µF common-mode capacitor — never to VDDAT or any supply rail. Tying a center tap to a power rail instead of ground biases the transformer incorrectly and degrades signal quality.

On the line side, a Bob-Smith termination is sometimes added: ~75Ω from the center tap to chassis ground through a 1000 pF / 2kV-rated capacitor, improving common-mode EMI rejection.

MDI — trace geometry

Differential pair routing rules

Every TX/RX pair between PHY and magnetics is a 100Ω differential pair. The geometry is what actually holds that impedance — not just the resistor values elsewhere on the board.

  • Length matching: keep +/− within ~50 mils of each other — skew becomes common-mode noise and hurts return loss.
  • Constant spacing: don't let the pair drift wider or narrower along its run; that changes impedance and creates reflection points.
  • Ground clearance on the same layer: at least 3× the trace width, 5× preferred, to avoid unwanted capacitive loading.
  • Continuous reference plane directly beneath the pair for its whole length — except where intentionally voided.
  • Plane void under magnetics, ESD diodes, AC-coupling caps — prevents return current from bridging chassis and signal ground through the plane itself. Automotive designs void two full layers here, not just one.
  • 45° or curved corners only near ESD-sensitive nets — sharp 90° corners are impedance discontinuities and minor antennas.

D1–D8 — connector-side protection

ESD protection at the jack

The transformer provides inherent isolation, but real designs add explicit protection right at the connector:

  • Bidirectional TVS diode arrays across each differential pair, placed as close to the RJ45 as possible — before the signal travels any distance across the board.
  • Short, direct trace from connector to TVS — ESD is a sub-nanosecond transient; a long trace lets the pulse couple into neighboring traces before it's clamped.
  • Dedicated, unshared ground return for the TVS — a shared via means the ESD pulse's brief but intense localized magnetic field can couple into whatever else uses that via.
  • Large chassis-ground footprint (often SMD_1210 or larger) at the single bridge point to digital ground — chosen for low impedance at ESD-relevant frequencies.
Unidirectional vs bidirectional TVS — which and why?
Unidirectional TVS diodes suit DC rails that only swing one polarity. Ethernet's differential pairs swing above and below ground in normal operation, so bidirectional TVS devices are required there — a unidirectional part would clip half of every legitimate signal swing, not just ESD events.
Y1 — oscillator sizing

Load capacitor calculation

The two load caps on X1/X2 aren't arbitrary values — they're derived from the crystal's rated load capacitance (CL, typically 10–20pF, given on the crystal's own datasheet):

C_load_cap ≈ 2 × (C_L − C_stray)
// C_stray: board + pin parasitic capacitance, commonly assumed ~3–5pF

Example: crystal rated at 18pF load capacitance, ~4pF stray → (18−4)×2 ≈ 28pF split across two caps. In practice, most designs just use the crystal vendor's app-note recommendation directly — commonly landing in the 15–22pF range — since stray capacitance is board-specific and hard to predict exactly by hand.

  • Drive level: confirm the crystal's rated drive level is compatible (commonly ~100µW or higher) — too low relative to what the internal oscillator expects can cause unstable startup.
  • Physical placement: keep the crystal and both load caps close to X1/X2 with a short, direct ground return — this loop is a free-running RF oscillator, one of the more EMI-sensitive spots on the whole board.

signal layer ground reference power plane

The core discipline: every high-speed differential pair keeps an uninterrupted reference plane on the very next layer down. If a trace must change layers, route the transition through vias placed near a ground-stitching via, so return current has a low-inductance path to follow the signal across the layer change.

TP1–TPn — bring-up provisions

Designing for bring-up, not just function

  • Test points on RST_N, the 25MHz clock (if accessible), and each strap pin — verify reset timing and boot-time configuration with a scope before assuming misbehavior is a firmware bug.
  • 0Ω resistor options on ambiguous straps instead of hard-wired pull-up/pull-down — a guessed-wrong mode (MII vs RMII on Port 5) becomes a rework, not a redesign.
  • Unpopulated LED footprints even if not planned for production — since those pins double as straps, having the pads present (DNP'd) gives visibility during bring-up without committing board space permanently.
