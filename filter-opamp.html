01

Filter Circuits

frequency-selective networks

THEORYPassive vs Active Filters

A filter is a frequency-selective circuit — it treats some frequencies differently than others.

Passive (R, L, C only)

  • Built purely from resistors, capacitors, inductors — no power supply required.
  • Work at any signal level, including RF, since there's no active device to saturate or slew-limit.
  • Gain is always ≤ 1 (0 dB) — a passive network can only attenuate, never amplify.
  • Output impedance is non-zero, so the very next stage loads the filter and shifts its cutoff/shape — you must buffer or account for loading.
  • Inductors get physically large and lossy at low audio/instrumentation frequencies, so most low-frequency passive filters are RC, not RLC.

Best at: RF filtering, mains/EMI filtering, simple one-stage jobs where a little roll-off is enough.

Active (op-amp + R, C)

  • The op-amp replaces the inductor entirely — no bulky, lossy magnetics.
  • Can provide gain while filtering (amplify and shape in one stage).
  • Low output impedance buffers the filter from whatever comes next — stages cascade cleanly without interaction.
  • Can realize much sharper roll-offs (2nd order and higher) in a small footprint.
  • Needs a power supply, and is bandwidth-limited by the op-amp's own gain-bandwidth product and slew rate.

Best at: audio and instrumentation, anti-aliasing ahead of an ADC, sensor conditioning.

The Four Basic Responses

Low-Pass (LPF): passes frequencies below the cutoff, attenuates above it.
High-Pass (HPF): passes above the cutoff, attenuates below it (mirror image of the LPF).
Band-Pass (BPF): passes only a window of frequencies between two cutoffs — built from a HPF and LPF in series.
Band-Stop / Notch: removes a narrow band (classic use: killing 50/60 Hz mains hum) while passing everything else.

All four can be built passive or active; the topology (which element goes to ground vs. in series) is what decides which response you get.

CIRCUITRC Low-Pass Filter

Passes low frequencies, rolls off the highs — the single most common filter in electronics.

Cutoff & Uses

f_c = 1 / (2πRC)

With R = 1.6 kΩ and C = 100 nF, f_c ≈ 1 kHz.

  • At f_c the output is −3 dB, i.e. 70.7% of input amplitude, and the output lags the input by 45°.
  • Roll-off beyond f_c is −20 dB/decade (a first-order filter has exactly one reactive element).
  • Uses: anti-aliasing ahead of an ADC, smoothing a PWM output into a DC level, audio tone/treble controls, general noise removal.

Why the −3 dB / 45° Point

The circuit is a voltage divider between R and the capacitor's impedance Z_C = 1/(jωC). At f_c, ω = 1/RC, so |Z_C| = R exactly. Two equal impedances in a divider always split the input by 1/√2 in magnitude (−3 dB) and by 45° in phase — that's a geometric fact of the divider, not a coincidence of RC filters specifically.

Flat through the passband → knee at 1 kHz → straight −20 dB/decade slope on log-frequency axis

Reading the Graph — region by region

  • f ≪ f_c (flat, 0 dB): capacitor impedance is huge compared to R, so almost none of V_in is dropped across R — V_out ≈ V_in.
  • f = f_c (the knee, −3 dB): R and Z_C are equal magnitude — the divider gives exactly 1/√2.
  • f ≫ f_c (straight-line roll-off): Z_C keeps shrinking as frequency rises (Z_C = 1/(2πfC)), so more and more of V_in is dropped across R. Because the plot's x-axis is logarithmic, a response that's inversely proportional to frequency becomes a straight line, falling 20 dB for every ×10 in frequency — this is why the curve looks bent on a linear axis but perfectly straight past the knee on a log/dB (Bode) plot.

CIRCUITRC Band-Pass Filter

High-pass + low-pass in cascade = a frequency window.

Key Numbers & Uses

Passband: f_L = 300 Hz to f_H = 3 kHz

f_0 = √(f_L × f_H) ≈ 950 Hz
BW = f_H − f_L    Q = f_0 / BW

Uses: radio tuning front-ends, audio EQ bands, extracting a sensor's carrier tone out of noise, and the classic telephone voice band.

Reading the Graph

Rises at +20 dB/decade below f_L (the HPF stage dominates there), stays roughly flat through the window between f_L and f_H, then falls at −20 dB/decade above f_H (the LPF stage dominates). The result is a hill, not a cliff, because each stage is only first-order — a sharper "boxier" band needs higher-order (active) stages.

Rising edge (HPF, +20 dB/dec) → flat window → falling edge (LPF, −20 dB/dec)

CIRCUITActive Filters — Sallen-Key

Adding an op-amp: gain, buffering, and a sharper roll-off.

Why Go Active?

Cascading passive RC stages back-to-back doesn't multiply their response cleanly — each stage loads the previous one and the combined response goes "soggy" (loses its sharp knee). An op-amp isolates the stages (near-infinite input impedance, near-zero output impedance) and its feedback network can be arranged to sharpen the corner instead of softening it.

A 2nd-order Sallen-Key stage rolls off at −40 dB/decade — twice as steep as a single RC — with zero inductors.

Response Families (how the poles are placed)

  • Butterworth — maximally flat passband, monotonic roll-off. The general-purpose default when you just want "flat then falls."
  • Chebyshev — steeper transition band for the same order, at the cost of passband ripple.
  • Bessel — best preserves pulse/step shape (nearly constant group delay) at the cost of a gentler knee. Use for pulse and time-domain-critical signals.

Design tools: TI Filter Designer, Analog Devices Filter Wizard — these turn f_c/Q/order into resistor and capacitor values automatically.

Result

−40 dB/decade with no inductor. Using equal-value parts (R1=R2, C1=C2 or a fixed ratio) gives a simple design equation f_c = 1/(2πRC), with Q set by the C1/C2 (or R1/R2) ratio — Q controls how peaked vs. flat the response is right around the knee.

Same cutoff, twice the slope — the 2nd-order curve reaches the noise floor a full decade sooner

02

Amplifiers

op-amp basics · inverting & non-inverting

THEORYWhat Is an Amplifier? The Op-Amp

Small signal in, faithful bigger copy out.

Gain

A_v = V_out / V_in

In decibels: dB = 20 × log10(A_v). ×10 gain = 20 dB, ×100 gain = 40 dB — every ×10 in voltage gain adds 20 dB.

The op-amp itself is a differential-input amplifier with a huge open-loop gain (100 dB+, i.e. often >100,000×). By itself that gain is unusably large and unpredictable (it varies with temperature, supply, part-to-part) — feedback resistors around it tame that raw gain down to a precise, repeatable value.

The Two Golden Rules

With negative feedback in place, every op-amp circuit obeys:

  1. The inputs draw no current (input impedance is treated as infinite).
  2. The output does whatever is needed to force V(+) = V(−).

Every inverting, non-inverting, differential, follower, and integrator circuit is just these two rules applied to a different resistor network — memorize the rules, not the formulas.

Gain × Bandwidth = Constant

The open-loop curve rolls off at −20 dB/decade from a very low corner frequency (often only a few Hz) all the way up to the point where gain hits 1 (0 dB) — that frequency is the op-amp's Gain-Bandwidth Product (GBW), a fixed spec for a given part (e.g. 1 MHz, 10 MHz…). Set a closed-loop gain of 40 dB (×100) and that flat line rides along until it intersects the open-loop slope — at that intersection point the amplifier runs out of loop gain to sustain the response, and it starts rolling off too. Ask for less gain and you get more usable bandwidth for free; ask for more gain and bandwidth shrinks — the product of the two stays constant for a given op-amp.

CIRCUITOp-Amp Amplifier Circuits

The two workhorse gain stages — everything else is a variation on these.

Inverting Amplifier

A_v = −R_f / R_in

Output is inverted (180° phase flip). The − input sits at a virtual ground — held at 0 V by feedback even though it's never physically tied to ground. Input impedance is limited to R_in (a real, finite load on the source).

Non-Inverting Amplifier

A_v = 1 + R_f/R_g

High input impedance (signal drives the + input directly, which draws no current). Gain can never be less than 1. Output is in-phase with the input.

Which One?

Inverting: precise gain, a virtual-ground summing point (great for mixers/summing amps), but its input impedance equals R_in — it does load the source. Non-inverting: near-infinite input impedance — use when the source is weak or high-impedance (sensors, resistive dividers). Both set gain purely by a resistor ratio, so gain accuracy depends only on resistor tolerance/matching, not on the op-amp's own (imprecise) open-loop gain.

Differential Amp, Follower & Schmitt Trigger

three op-amp circuits every engineer uses

CIRCUITDifferential Amplifier

Amplifies the DIFFERENCE between two inputs, rejects what both inputs share.

What It Does

V_out = (R_f/R1) × (V2 − V1)

Requires matched resistor pairs (R1 = R2, R_f = R_g) — any mismatch lets common-mode signal leak into the output as an error.

Common-mode signals — anything identical on both inputs, like 50/60 Hz hum picked up equally by both wires, or a shared ground offset — get subtracted away rather than amplified. CMRR (Common-Mode Rejection Ratio) rates how well: typical values run 80–120 dB.

Where It Is Used

  • Sensor bridges — strain gauges, load cells
  • Current-sense amplifiers (reading the tiny drop across a shunt — see Section 4)
  • Balanced audio inputs (XLR)
  • Instrumentation amplifier = three op-amps built around this core, adding very high input impedance plus single-resistor gain adjustment

Why the ratio matters: both inputs go through matched resistor pairs before reaching the op-amp. If a voltage rises equally on V1 and V2 (common-mode), it produces the same effect on both the + and − paths and cancels in the subtraction. Only the piece where V2 and V1 disagree survives, scaled by R_f/R1. This is why resistor matching is the dominant real-world limitation — a 1% mismatch between "matched" resistors directly leaks 1%-ish of the common-mode signal into your differential output.

CIRCUITVoltage Follower (Unity-Gain Buffer)

Gain = 1 — so what is it for? Impedance transformation.

The Point of Gain = 1

  • Input impedance: essentially infinite — draws virtually no current from a weak source.
  • Output impedance: near zero — drives heavy loads without sagging.

It copies a voltage while isolating the source from the load — the source "sees" almost no load, and the load "sees" a low-impedance, muscular driver.

Classic Uses

  • High-Z sensor or voltage-divider output → ADC input (the sample-and-hold capacitor needs to charge fast, which needs a low-impedance driver)
  • Between filter stages, to stop one RC stage from loading the previous one
  • Driving long cables/traces
  • Reference-voltage distribution

Parts: almost any general-purpose op-amp works — MCP6001, TL072, OPA340 are common choices.

Golden Rule 2 in Action

The output moves until V(−) = V(+) = V_in. Since the output is V(−) directly (no divider in the feedback path), V_out = V_in exactly — but now that voltage is backed by the op-amp's output stage, able to supply real current instead of the weak source's trickle.

CIRCUITSchmitt Trigger

A comparator with memory — two thresholds instead of one.

How: Positive Feedback

A comparator plus a feedback divider that shifts its own threshold: after switching high, the threshold drops — the input must fall much further to switch back. The gap between the rising and falling threshold is called hysteresis.

Hysteresis

Output flips HIGH only above V_TH (upper threshold) and back LOW only below V_TL (lower threshold) — the gap between them swallows any noise smaller than (V_TH − V_TL). A plain comparator with a single threshold would chatter (flip repeatedly) every time noisy input wiggled across that one level; the Schmitt trigger's dead-band prevents that entirely.

Orange = noisy analog input · Green = clean digital output. Output flips HIGH only once input climbs past 2.0 V, and back LOW only once it drops below 1.0 V.

The Shunt

measuring current by reading a tiny voltage

CIRCUITShunt Resistor — Current Sensing

You can't measure current directly with a voltage-reading ADC — a shunt converts current into a voltage you can read.

How It Works

A very small, precise resistor (milliohm range) is placed in the current path itself.

Ohm's law does the measuring:

V_shunt = I × R_shunt

Example: 10 A × 10 mΩ = 100 mV, dissipating P = I²R = 1 W in the resistor. That last part matters — this is a power resistor, not a signal resistor.

Low-Side vs High-Side

Low-side (shunt in the ground return): simple, directly ADC-friendly since the sensed voltage sits near ground — but the load's own ground lifts by V_shunt, and a short-to-chassis fault downstream of the shunt goes unseen.

High-side (shunt in the supply lead): needs a current-sense amplifier (e.g. INA219, INA226) that can tolerate a large common-mode voltage, but it sees every fault on the load, including shorts to ground.

Kelvin (4-Wire) Connection

Separate sense wires tap the shunt element directly — right at the resistive element, not at the current-carrying terminals — so that lead and contact resistance (which can rival the shunt's own milliohm-scale value) adds zero error to the measurement. Skipping the Kelvin connection is one of the most common sources of "my current reading drifts with cable length/temperature" bugs.

Shunt & Current-Sense Selection Checklist

  • Shunt value: trade-off between measurable signal (bigger R_sh → bigger V_shunt, easier to read) and power loss/voltage drop in the power path (bigger R_sh → more wasted power, more IR drop). Aim for a full-scale drop typically in the 50–100 mV range for dedicated current-sense amplifiers.
  • Power rating: P = I²R at max current, with real design margin (2–3×) — shunts run hot, and resistance drifts with self-heating.
  • Temperature coefficient (TCR): low-TCR alloys (manganin, Zeraust, Evenohm) hold their value as they self-heat; a cheap general resistor's value can drift several percent across its own temperature rise.
  • 4-terminal (Kelvin) package: use dedicated 4-terminal shunts for anything beyond a rough estimate — 2-terminal shunts fold lead/solder resistance directly into your reading.
  • Sense amplifier CMRR and common-mode range: especially critical for high-side sensing, where the sense amp must tolerate the full supply voltage riding on both inputs while still resolving a millivolt-scale differential signal.
  • Bandwidth vs. accuracy: dedicated ICs (INA219/INA226/INA240 family) trade off sample rate, offset voltage, gain accuracy, and whether they include a built-in ADC.
  • Inductance: for fast-switching currents (e.g. inside a switching regulator), shunt and trace inductance can add a spike on top of the true resistive signal — low-inductance shunt geometries matter for pulsed/PWM current sensing.

Practical Notes

Families: 74HC (CMOS, 2–6 V) is the default choice today. Unused inputs must always be tied to a rail — never left floating (a floating CMOS input picks up noise and can even damage the part). Propagation delay is roughly 10 ns per 74HC gate stage. Schmitt-input gates (74HC14) clean up slow/noisy edges before they reach digital logic. In modern designs, small MCU pins with software logic often replace what used to be discrete gate chips.

From gates to everything: Gates → adders & latches → registers & ALUs → the CPU core. Every digital chip on the market is fundamentally just these six gate types, repeated billions of times over.

Component Selection Guide

what to actually check on a datasheet

The formulas above tell you what value a component needs. This section covers the real-world parasitics and datasheet specs that decide which actual part to buy — the difference between a circuit that works on paper and one that works on the bench.

SELECTOp-Amps

Parameters to check on every op-amp datasheet

  • Gain-Bandwidth Product (GBW): must comfortably exceed (closed-loop gain × highest signal frequency you need); rule of thumb, pick GBW at least 10× your required signal bandwidth × gain.
  • Slew rate (V/µs): the fastest the output can change, independent of GBW. A signal that's within bandwidth on paper can still come out triangle-wave-distorted ("slew limited") if the slew rate is too low for its amplitude and frequency — check SR ≥ 2π·f·V_peak.
  • Input offset voltage (V_OS) and drift (µV/°C): the effective DC error at the input. Matters hugely in precision/instrumentation work (differential amps, shunt sensing) and largely irrelevant in a simple audio buffer.
  • Input bias current / offset current: current the input pins draw. Combined with source impedance, this creates an additional offset voltage error (I_bias × R_source) — important with high-impedance sensors.
  • CMRR & PSRR: rejection of common-mode input swings and of supply-rail noise respectively — critical for differential amps and anything running from a noisy or shared rail.
  • Input/output voltage range — rail-to-rail or not: a non-rail-to-rail op-amp can't swing its input or output all the way to the supply rails, which matters a lot on single-supply (e.g. 0–3.3 V or 0–5 V) designs.
  • Output current drive: how much current the output stage can source/sink before it sags — matters when driving low-impedance loads (speakers, LEDs, long cables).
  • Noise density (nV/√Hz, pA/√Hz): critical for low-level sensor front-ends; largely ignorable for switching/logic-level work.
  • Supply voltage range and quiescent current: must fit your rail, and quiescent current matters directly for battery-powered designs.
  • Single, dual, or quad package; unity-gain stability: some fast/high-gain op-amps are only stable above a minimum closed-loop gain — check the datasheet before using one as a unity-gain follower.

SELECTResistors

Parameters to check

  • Tolerance: ±1% metal film is the practical default for anything gain-setting or filter-defining; ±5% carbon film is fine for non-critical pull-ups/current-limiting.
  • Temperature coefficient (ppm/°C): matters for matched pairs (differential amps, precision dividers) and for anything that self-heats.
  • Power rating: derate — don't run a resistor near its rated power continuously; picking the next package size up (or splitting current between two resistors) buys long-term reliability.
  • Matching, not just absolute tolerance: for differential/instrumentation circuits, what matters is how well two resistors track each other, not just each one's individual accuracy — matched resistor networks or arrays beat two random 1% parts.
  • Voltage rating and self-inductance: usually ignorable at audio/instrumentation frequencies, but wire-wound resistors add unwanted inductance at RF.

SELECTCapacitors

Parameters to check

  • Dielectric type: C0G/NP0 ceramics are the gold standard for filters and timing — stable value over voltage and temperature. X7R/X5R ceramics are cheaper/smaller but their capacitance shifts with applied DC voltage and temperature — avoid them where the exact cutoff frequency matters. Electrolytics/tantalums are for bulk decoupling and DC-blocking, not precision filtering.
  • Tolerance: ±5–10% is common for ceramics; tighter tolerance costs more and is rarely needed outside precision filters/oscillators.
  • Voltage rating and derating: ceramic capacitance actually drops as you approach the rated voltage (especially with X7R/X5R) — derate to roughly half the rated voltage for stable value.
  • ESR (Equivalent Series Resistance): matters for power-supply decoupling/filtering and switching circuits; largely negligible for small-signal RC filters.
  • Leakage current and dielectric absorption: matters for sample-and-hold and integrator circuits, where a "memory" effect in the dielectric can distort long time-constant behavior.

SELECTShunt Resistors & Current-Sense ICs

Recap — see Section 04 for full detail

  • Resistance value: balances signal size against power loss and voltage drop in the power path.
  • Power rating with real margin, plus low TCR so the value doesn't drift as it self-heats.
  • 4-terminal (Kelvin) construction to eliminate lead-resistance error.
  • Low inductance for fast/PWM current waveforms.
  • Matching sense-amplifier CMRR/common-mode range to low-side vs high-side placement.

SELECTLogic Gate Families & ICs

Parameters to check

  • Logic family / supply voltage: 74HC(T) CMOS (2–6 V) is the general default; 74LVC for low-voltage (1.65–3.6 V) modern designs; legacy 74LS(TTL) still shows up in older designs. Never mix families casually — voltage thresholds differ.
  • Noise margin: the gap between a valid output level and the next stage's input threshold; CMOS families generally have wider margins than TTL.
  • Propagation delay: how long after an input change the output responds — stacks up across cascaded gates and can matter for timing-critical logic.
  • Fan-out / drive strength: how many downstream gate inputs (or how much load current) one output can reliably drive.
  • Schmitt-trigger inputs (the "14" suffix, e.g. 74HC14): choose these specifically when an input signal is slow-edged or noisy — a plain logic gate's ill-defined switching region can cause oscillation/metastability on a slow edge.
  • Unused-input handling: always tie unused inputs to a defined level (VCC or GND) — a floating CMOS input is a high-impedance node that picks up noise, can oscillate, and can pull unnecessary current or even damage the part.
  • Package & gate count: single/dual/quad/hex gate packages trade board area against routing convenience.
The one habit that prevents most of these problems: before finalizing any component choice, ask "what happens to this value across temperature, tolerance, and aging — and does my circuit still work at the worst-case combination?" That's the real job of component selection: not picking the part that's exactly right in the typical case, but the one that's still good enough at the corners.
