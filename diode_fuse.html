Selection Guide & Parameters

Diodes & Protection Devices

A working reference for selecting and applying diodes, TVS/GDT, Zeners, thermistors, and fuses in real circuit design.

This expands your six-section deck into full engineering depth — the physics, the datasheet parameters that actually matter, the worked math, and the application circuits with the reasoning behind each choice. Read it once top to bottom, then keep it as a lookup table when you're picking parts.

01The Diode

A PN junction — the one-way valve every other part in this guide is a variation of.

Physics: what actually happens at the junction

A diode is two pieces of silicon fused together — one doped with extra electrons (N-type) and one doped with electron "holes" (P-type). Where they meet, electrons and holes diffuse across and cancel each other out, leaving a thin zone with no free carriers: the depletion region. This region acts like an insulator and, because the charges that created it are fixed dopant ions, it also builds a small built-in electric field (~0.6–0.7 V for silicon) that opposes further diffusion.

Forward bias (Anode +, Cathode −)

The external field pushes against the built-in field, shrinking the depletion region. Once the applied voltage exceeds the built-in potential (~0.6–0.7 V for Si, ~0.2–0.3 V for Ge, ~0.15–0.45 V for Schottky), carriers flood across and current rises exponentially with voltage — this is the diode equation region.

Reverse bias (Cathode +, Anode −)

The external field adds to the built-in field, widening the depletion region. Only a tiny leakage current flows (thermally generated minority carriers) — nanoamps to microamps at room temperature, roughly doubling every 10 °C. This holds until the breakdown voltage (VRRM/VBR), where the field becomes strong enough to rip electrons loose (avalanche) or tunnel them through the barrier (Zener effect in heavily doped junctions).

Shockley diode equation: I = I_s · (e^(V / (n·V_T)) − 1) I_s = reverse saturation current (device-specific, temp-dependent) n = ideality factor (~1 for ideal, 1–2 for real silicon diodes) V_T = thermal voltage = kT/q ≈ 25.85 mV at 300 K

Where diodes are actually used — and why that topology was chosen

Rectification (AC → DC)

Half-wave uses 1 diode but wastes half the AC cycle and leaves large ripple. Full-wave bridge (4 diodes) uses both halves of the cycle, doubling ripple frequency and halving the capacitor size needed for the same ripple voltage. Every mains-powered supply uses this.
Parts: 1N4007, GBU806

Freewheeling / flyback

An inductor (relay coil, motor winding) fights any sudden change in current. When the driving transistor switches off, the coil's collapsing field tries to force current to keep flowing — generating a huge inductive voltage spike that can exceed the transistor's breakdown voltage instantly. A diode across the coil (cathode to +V) gives that current a path to recirculate harmlessly instead of arcing through the switch.

Reverse polarity protection

A series diode in the supply line simply won't conduct if the battery is inserted backwards, protecting everything downstream. The cost is a permanent V_F drop and I²·V_F power loss — which is why designers often swap in a Schottky (lower V_F) or even a P-channel MOSFET (near-zero drop) when efficiency matters.

Clamping & clipping

Diodes to the rails (input protection on ICs) catch any voltage that swings more than ~0.7 V above V_CC or below GND and shunt the excess current into the supply rail instead of letting it punch through gate oxide. Dual-diode packages like BAV99 are the standard implementation.

OR-ing / power select

Two diodes, cathodes tied together at the load, anodes to two different supplies (battery + wall adapter). Whichever source is higher wins automatically and the lower source is blocked from back-feeding — a purely passive power mux. Downside: you pay a V_F drop on whichever source is active, which is why "ideal diode" controller ICs (using MOSFETs) replace this in efficiency-critical designs.

LED (light-emitting diode)

Same PN-junction physics, but the semiconductor bandgap is chosen so recombination releases a photon instead of just heat. V_F is much higher and depends on color (1.8 V red → 3.4 V blue/white) because bandgap energy sets photon energy sets color. LEDs have no useful reverse breakdown rating, so they always need a series current-limiting resistor: R = (V_supply − V_F) / I_LED.

Full-wave bridge rectifier
D1/D2 (blue) route current from either AC terminal into DC+. D3/D4 (green) return current from DC− back to whichever AC terminal is currently negative. Each half-cycle a different diode pair conducts, but the output polarity across C and RL never flips.
Flyback (freewheeling) diode across a relay coil
Diode cathode faces V+, anode faces the switch node — reverse-biased and invisible while the switch is on. When the switch opens, the collapsing coil field forward-biases the diode, giving the current a safe loop back into V+ instead of spiking across the switch.
Reverse polarity protection
If the battery is inserted backwards, the diode simply doesn't conduct — no current reaches the load at all. Costs one V_F drop and I·V_F of heat in normal operation, which is why a Schottky or a MOSFET is often substituted when that loss matters.
Engineer's rule of thumb For fast hand-analysis, treat a forward-conducting silicon diode as a fixed 0.7 V drop. Remember V_F drifts down about 2 mV/°C as the junction heats — this matters when you're paralleling diodes (thermal runaway risk, see Schottky section) or designing precision references.

Cathode identification

On a real part, the painted band or stripe on the body marks the cathode end — current flows into the anode and out the cathode when forward biased. Common reference parts: 1N4148 (small-signal switching), 1N4007 (1 A rectifier, 1000 V rated).

02Schottky Diode

Metal–semiconductor junction — low forward voltage, essentially zero reverse recovery time.

What makes it different

Instead of a P-N junction, a Schottky diode is formed by a metal (aluminum, platinum silicide, etc.) deposited directly onto N-type silicon. Current flows via majority carriers only (electrons in the N-region moving into the metal) — there's no minority-carrier injection and therefore no stored charge to remove when you switch it off. That single structural difference explains both of its headline advantages:

Lower V_F

Typically 0.15–0.45 V vs 0.6–0.7 V for silicon PN. Less energy barrier to cross at the metal-semiconductor interface. Directly cuts conduction losses — critical in high-current rectification and battery-powered gear.

Near-zero reverse recovery

No minority carrier storage means turn-off is governed only by junction capacitance discharge, not carrier sweep-out. This lets Schottkys switch cleanly into the MHz range where a regular PN diode's recovery current would cause huge switching losses and EMI.

The trade-offs

What you give up
  • Higher reverse leakage (I_R) — grows significantly with temperature, and in the worst case can lead to thermal runaway: leakage heats the junction, higher temperature increases leakage further. This is why Schottkys are derated more conservatively at high T and why paralleling them for higher current needs care (current-sharing resistors or matched parts).
  • Lower reverse voltage rating — most Schottkys top out well under 200 V, vs kV-class silicon rectifiers, because the same structure that gives low V_F also limits achievable breakdown voltage.

Where Schottkys are chosen deliberately

  • Switching power supply rectification (buck/boost freewheeling diode) — the combination of low V_F (efficiency) and fast recovery (high switching frequency, small magnetics) is exactly what SMPS needs.
  • Reverse-polarity protection where efficiency matters — same series-diode topology as a regular diode, but the lower V_F means less voltage headroom and heat lost, especially valuable in low-voltage battery systems (3.3 V / 5 V rails).
  • RF and mixer/detector diodes — fast switching plus low turn-on voltage makes them useful at signal levels a silicon PN junction can't respond to cleanly.
  • Solar panel bypass and blocking diodes — low V_F minimizes wasted power in a system where every fraction of a volt matters.
Design note Because Schottky leakage rises fast with both temperature and reverse voltage, always check I_R at your actual worst-case V_R and T_j on the datasheet curve — not just the room-temperature spec headline. In a hot enclosure near its V_RRM limit, a Schottky can leak enough to matter for power budget or even runaway.

03TVS Diode, GDT & ESD Protection

Clamping transients before they reach your circuit.

What a TVS diode is

A Transient Voltage Suppressor is essentially a Zener diode engineered for one job: absorb a huge, brief energy pulse without dying, by avalanching extremely fast and clamping the voltage across itself. It sits in parallel with the line it protects (not in series, like a rectifier) — under normal operation it's invisible (just leakage current); when a transient hits, it avalanches in under a nanosecond and diverts the surge current to ground/return, holding the protected node's voltage down while the energy dumps through the TVS instead of through your IC.

TVS placement — always parallel, right at the connector
Normal voltage: TVS is invisible (µA leakage). Transient arrives: TVS avalanches in <1 ns and shunts the surge current straight to ground, holding the IC's input near V_C instead of letting the full transient reach it.

Key parameters

  • V_RWM — standoff (max continuous working) voltage. Must be ≥ your line's max normal voltage, or the TVS itself will conduct and burn power during normal operation.
  • V_BR — breakdown voltage, tested at a small reference current (often 1 mA)
  • V_C — clamping voltage at the rated peak pulse current I_PP — this is the actual voltage your downstream IC will see during the worst-case surge
  • I_PP / P_PPM — peak pulse current / power the device can survive for the rated pulse waveform (commonly 8/20 µs or 10/1000 µs)
  • Response time — sub-nanosecond; this is what makes TVS diodes, not GDTs or MOVs, the last line of defense right at the IC

Unidirectional vs bidirectional

Unidirectional (single junction, arrow + bar symbol): clamps one polarity like a Zener; conducts normally like a regular diode in the other direction (~0.7 V). Correct for DC power lines where polarity is fixed.

Bidirectional (two junctions back-to-back): clamps both polarities symmetrically. Required for AC lines, audio signals, and differential data pairs (RS-485) where the signal legitimately swings both positive and negative.

Parts: SMBJ / SMAJ series, PESD, ESD9 arrays. ESD test standard: IEC 61000-4-2.

Worked selection example — protecting a 12 V automotive line

Module rated 30 V absolute maximum.

Step 1 — Standoff voltage

Rule: V_RWM ≥ maximum normal line voltage. A 12 V automotive rail peaks around 13.8 V while charging, so choose V_RWM ≥ 14 V.
Candidate: SMBJ14A (V_RWM = 14 V)

Step 2 — Breakdown & clamping

SMBJ14A: V_BR = 15.6–17.2 V (min–max). At the rated pulse current I_PP = 25.9 A, V_C = 23.2 V.
Check: V_C (23.2 V) < the module's absolute max (30 V) → 6.8 V margin, selection is safe.

Step 3 — Pulse power

SMBJ series is rated 600 W for a standard 10/1000 µs pulse. Verify the surge energy fits: P_pulse = V_C × I_PP = 23.2 × 25.9 ≈ 600 W ✓. For bigger surges, step up to SMCJ (1.5 kW) or 5KP (5 kW) series.

Uni or bi? A-suffix?

This is a DC line → unidirectional (SMBJ14A). An AC/audio/RS-485 pair would need bidirectional (SMBJ14CA). The "A" suffix means a tighter ±5% V_BR tolerance — prefer it whenever available for tighter, more predictable clamping.

GDT — the heavy artillery in front of the TVS

A Gas Discharge Tube is a sealed spark gap filled with inert gas. It doesn't clamp gently — it fires: once its spark-over voltage is exceeded, the gas ionizes and the tube's voltage collapses to a low arc voltage (~20 V), diverting enormous currents (5–20 kA surges) to ground almost for free. The trade-off is speed and precision: it's slow (~1 µs to fire) and its trigger voltage is high and imprecise (75–600 V range), so it's useless for protecting a modern IC directly — a 5 V logic pin would be destroyed long before a 200 V-rated GDT ever fires.

Coordinated / staged protection The standard architecture in telecom, Ethernet, and RS-485 interfaces is two stages: a GDT at the line entry absorbs the bulk energy of lightning or power-cross events, and a TVS at the IC gives the fast, precise final clamp. A series impedance (resistor or inductor) is placed between the two stages — this is essential, because it slows the initial edge just enough that the GDT has time to fire and take over the current before the TVS is forced to absorb energy it isn't rated for. Without that impedance, the fast TVS clamps first, the GDT never fires, and the TVS alone eats a surge sized for the GDT.
Two-stage coordinated protection
GDT takes the kiloamps at the line entry; the series resistor limits the current the fast TVS ever sees and buys the GDT time to fire; the TVS clamps precisely right at the IC. Used in telecom, Ethernet, and RS-485 line interfaces.

Response time comparison — why the order matters

DeviceResponse timeBehavior

Layout matters as much as part choice Every millimeter of PCB trace adds parasitic inductance, and that inductance creates its own voltage overshoot (V = L·di/dt) on top of whatever the TVS clamps to — a fast, high-current ESD edge through even a short lead can add several extra volts of spike right at the IC. Keep TVS leads and ground return paths as short as physically possible.

ESD test levels (IEC 61000-4-2)

Level 4, the toughest common consumer/industrial standard, requires surviving ±8 kV contact discharge and ±15 kV air discharge. The edge of that pulse rises in about 1 ns — only a TVS is fast enough to catch it before the pulse propagates into the IC. Multi-line ESD protection arrays (ESD9, TPD4E series) apply this same principle to buses with many pins at once — USB, HDMI, antenna interfaces.

04Zener Diode

A diode designed to work in reverse breakdown, on purpose.

How it works

Forward biased, a Zener behaves exactly like a normal diode (~0.7 V drop). The entire point of the part is its reverse behavior: instead of avoiding breakdown like a regular diode, a Zener is engineered — via heavy, precise doping — to break down at a specific, repeatable voltage V_Z (available from about 2.4 V to 200 V across the product family) and to hold that voltage nearly constant across a wide current range. It must always be current-limited by a series resistor — without one, nothing limits current once breakdown starts, and the part self-destructs instantly.

The two breakdown mechanisms — and why they matter

V_Z < 5 V — Zener tunnelling

The junction is so heavily doped, and the depletion region so thin, that electrons quantum-mechanically tunnel straight through the barrier at a relatively low field. This mechanism has a negative temperature coefficient — breakdown voltage drops as temperature rises.

V_Z > 5 V — Avalanche

At higher voltages the field accelerates carriers hard enough to knock loose additional electron-hole pairs on collision, which get accelerated too — a multiplying cascade. This has a positive temperature coefficient — breakdown voltage rises as temperature rises.

Why 5.6–6.2 V parts are special Right around V_Z ≈ 5.6–6.2 V, both mechanisms are present simultaneously and their opposite temperature coefficients cancel out. This gives near-zero tempco — the most temperature-stable reverse breakdown available — which is exactly why this voltage class is the default choice whenever you need a stable voltage reference, not just a clamp.

Reading the I–V curve

On the reverse side, the curve is flat and near-zero current all the way from 0 V to just past −V_Z, then turns into a near-vertical wall — voltage barely moves even as current changes by an order of magnitude. That vertical wall is the regulation behavior: any excess current from a fluctuating input gets absorbed while the voltage across the Zener stays essentially fixed. The forward side is a completely normal diode knee at +0.7 V.

Worked regulator circuit

Zener shunt regulator
R_S drops the excess (Vin − Vz) and sets Zener current: I = (9 − 5.1) / 330 ≈ 12 mA. R_L sees a fixed 5.1 V as long as the Zener stays in its regulation region.
V_in = 9 V, target V_out = V_Z = 5.1 V, R_S = 330 Ω I = (V_in − V_Z) / R_S = (9 − 5.1) / 330 ≈ 12 mA

R_S does two jobs at once: it drops the excess voltage (V_in − V_Z) and, by Ohm's law, it sets the Zener current. Everything downstream (R_L) sees a fixed V_Z as long as the Zener stays in its regulation region — i.e. as long as load current draw doesn't starve the Zener of its minimum required current.

Design rules that actually matter

  • Keep I_Z above ~5 mA for a sharp, low-noise knee — operate a Zener too close to the bottom of its curve and regulation gets soft and noisy.
  • Check the power rating: P = V_Z × I_Z must stay under the part's rating (commonly 400 mW / 1 W / 5 W packages) — this is what actually limits how much current you can pull through it, and by extension how much load current variation the circuit can absorb.
  • Size R_S for the worst case: minimum load current (max I_Z through the Zener, check power) and maximum load current (I_Z must not drop below its minimum regulation current).

Where Zeners are actually used

  • Voltage reference — the 5.6–6.2 V near-zero-tempco parts, often followed by an op-amp buffer for a stable, load-independent reference.
  • Clamp / overvoltage protection — same avalanche behavior as a TVS but slower and lower power; fine for steady overvoltage, not fast transients.
  • Level shifting — a fixed V_Z offset dropped in series can shift a signal or supply rail by a known, stable amount.
  • Low-cost ESD-adjacent clamp — cheaper than a dedicated TVS but with nanosecond-class response rather than the TVS's sub-ns response, so it's used where the threat is steady overvoltage, not an ESD edge.

Parts: BZX55C5V1, 1N4733A.

05PTC & NTC Thermistors

Resistors that change dramatically with temperature — for very different reasons and very different jobs.

PTC (Positive Temperature Coefficient) — the resettable fuse

A polymer PTC (PPTC, "polyfuse") is a polymer matrix loaded with conductive carbon particles, forming countless tiny conductive chains through the material. Normally it's a low, stable resistance. When overcurrent forces enough self-heating, the polymer thermally expands — physically pulling the carbon chains apart — and resistance jumps 100–10,000× almost like a switch opening, choking the current down to a trickle. Critically, once the fault clears and the part cools, the polymer contracts, the chains reconnect, and the device resets itself automatically — no service call, no replacement.

Reading the R vs T curve Resistance stays flat and low from room temperature up to roughly the trip point (~110 °C in a typical part), then rockets upward almost vertically. That cliff — not a gradual slope — is what interrupts the fault current quickly once triggered.

Applications: USB port/hub overcurrent protection, battery pack protection (paired with cell protection ICs), motor and speaker protection. A related but different device, the ceramic PTC (based on doped barium titanate, a different physical mechanism than the polymer type), is used for self-regulating heaters, degaussing coils, and inrush limiting — it self-regulates temperature because rising resistance throttles its own self-heating current.

Parts: MF-R050, RXEF series.

NTC (Negative Temperature Coefficient) thermistor

A metal-oxide ceramic whose resistance falls exponentially as it heats — the opposite behavior, from a different mechanism entirely (more charge carriers become thermally available as temperature rises, similar in spirit to intrinsic semiconductor conduction).

R(T) = R25 · exp[ B · (1/T − 1/298) ] B (beta) ≈ 3435–3950 K typically, T in Kelvin 10 kΩ at 25 °C is the common standard reference value

Reading a real curve: a 10 kΩ (B=3950) part might run 97 kΩ at −20 °C and fall all the way to roughly 0.34 kΩ at 125 °C — a steep exponential fall that gives excellent sensitivity for temperature sensing, but also means you need a well-chosen fixed resistor in a voltage divider to get a usable, roughly-linear-enough output over your actual temperature range of interest.

Inrush current limiting

Placed in series with the input of a power supply. Cold at power-on, it presents high resistance and blocks the surge that would otherwise slam into a completely discharged bulk capacitor. As current flows continuously, self-heating drops its resistance toward ~0 Ω, so it becomes nearly invisible during normal running — the opposite tradeoff of a fuse, since it's not sacrificial and doesn't need current-limiting concern in steady state (though it does waste some power while hot).

Temperature sensing

Used in a voltage divider feeding an ADC in 3D printers (hotend/bed), battery packs (thermal protection cutoff), and HVAC systems. Also used for temperature compensation of other circuit parameters that drift with heat.

NTC inrush limiter at a PSU input
Cold at switch-on: NTC is high-resistance, so it absorbs the surge that would otherwise slam into the discharged bulk capacitor. Once running, self-heating drops its resistance near 0 Ω, so it wastes little power during steady operation.

Parts: NTCLE100E3, B57164K, SL22 5R0.

PTC vs NTC — don't mix them up under pressure PTC resistance rises with heat (used to interrupt current — a protective device). NTC resistance falls with heat (used to sense temperature, or to be low-resistance once warmed up for inrush limiting). Same "thermistor" family name, opposite curves, opposite jobs.

06Fuses

Sacrificial overcurrent protection.

How a fuse works

A calibrated metal element sized to melt open when current exceeds its rating for long enough — physically severing the circuit. It's a one-time, non-resettable device: once blown, it must be physically replaced (unlike a PTC). Speed grades trade blow-time against surge tolerance: FF (very fast), F (fast), T (slow-blow / time-delay), TT (very slow).

Key ratings to check on a datasheet

RatingMeaning
Current ratingContinuous carry current the fuse is designed to hold indefinitely (e.g. 2 A)
Voltage ratingThe maximum voltage the fuse can safely interrupt without arcing across the open gap — this is not just "line voltage," it must cover the voltage the fuse will actually see when it opens
Breaking capacityThe maximum fault current (kA class) the fuse can safely interrupt without rupturing or arcing catastrophically
I²tMelting energy — the actual physical quantity that determines when the element melts; must be matched against the inrush energy of the load so the fuse doesn't nuisance-trip on normal startup
Selection rule Size the fuse rating at roughly 1.25–1.5× the normal operating current — enough headroom to avoid nuisance blows from normal tolerance and inrush, tight enough to still protect the wiring and components downstream.

Reading the time–current curve

Blow time drops off steeply as overload increases — this is the defining characteristic of a fuse and the reason datasheets always plot it on log-log axes (a linear plot would be nearly useless to read). A typical curve: at 1.5× rated current the fuse might take on the order of minutes to open; by 10× rated current (a hard short) it opens in around 10 ms.

Fast-blow (F/FF)

Chosen to protect semiconductors — the fuse must open before the more fragile (and much faster-reacting) silicon downstream is damaged by the fault.

Slow-blow (T/TT)

Chosen to ride through the legitimate inrush of transformers, motors, and capacitor banks at power-on without nuisance-tripping, while still opening reliably on a genuine sustained overload.

Fuse vs PTC — which one?

FusePTC
Speed / precisionFast, preciseSlower
Fault current handledUp to kA classLimited
After a faultOne-shot, must replaceAuto-resetting

Rule of thumb: critical/mains circuits → fuse. User-facing ports like USB → PTC. Best designs often use both, layered.

IEC symbol: a box with the wire running straight through it.

Layered overcurrent protection — fuse + PTC in one supply path
Fuse handles a hard, high-current fault (kA-class) once, permanently. PTC downstream rides out smaller repeated overloads (like a shorted port) and resets itself once the fault clears — the two devices cover different parts of the fault spectrum in the same line.

Where fuses are used: mains input of every power supply, automotive blade fuses (one per circuit), battery packs (paired with PTC and a BMS), PCB-mount fuses for board-level fault isolation, HRC (high rupturing capacity) fuses in industrial distribution panels.

How all six devices fit together in one real design.

Layered defence — the mental model GDT (absorbs bulk surge energy) → fuse/PTC (limits sustained current) → TVS (fast, precise clamp) → Zener (on-chip level reference/clamp). Each stage handles what the next one can't: the GDT is slow but survives kA; the TVS is fast but can't survive kA; a fuse or PTC protects the copper and bulk components from sustained overcurrent that none of the transient-suppression parts are meant to carry indefinitely.

PTC hold current (I_hold)Must exceed your circuit's maximum normal operating current with margin, or the PTC nuisance-trips during legitimate useSizing only against typical current and getting nuisance trips at the high end of normal load variation
PTC trip current (I_trip)Must reliably trip below the current level that would damage downstream componentsLeaving too narrow a gap between hold and trip current — normal load spikes (not real faults) start tripping the part
PTC max voltage / interrupt ratingConfirm the part can safely interrupt at your line voltage — PTCs have voltage ratings just like fusesReusing a PTC rated for 5 V USB on a higher-voltage rail
NTC B-value & R25Choose R25 (resistance at 25 °C) and B-constant to give a usable, adequately sensitive voltage-divider output across your actual temperature range of interestPicking an NTC whose useful sensitivity range doesn't overlap your actual operating temperature window, giving a nearly flat, low-resolution reading
NTC self-heating (for inrush limiting)Check the resistance-vs-time recovery curve — an NTC that hasn't fully cooled after a quick power-cycle won't limit inrush the second timeAssuming the inrush limiter always starts "cold" — rapid on/off cycling can catch it still hot and under-protective

Fuses

ParameterWhat to checkCommon pitfall
Current ratingSize at roughly 1.25–1.5× normal operating current — high enough to avoid nuisance blows, low enough to still protect the wiring/componentsUndersizing against inrush (nuisance blows) or oversizing so far that it no longer protects anything downstream
Voltage ratingMust cover the voltage the fuse will actually see when it opens (which can exceed line voltage in inductive circuits) — arcing across an underrated fuse can be as dangerous as no fuse at allMatching only the DC line voltage and ignoring inductive kick or AC peak voltage at the moment of interruption
Breaking capacityConfirm the fuse can interrupt your system's maximum possible fault current (available short-circuit current), not just your normal operating currentA fuse can fail to open safely — or rupture — if the fault current exceeds its breaking capacity, even though its current rating looks "correct"
I²t (melting energy)Match against the load's actual inrush energy profile (motor start, transformer inrush, capacitor charge) so the fuse survives normal startup but still opens on a genuine sustained faultChoosing fast-blow where slow-blow inrush tolerance was actually needed, causing nuisance trips at every power-on
Speed grade (FF/F/T/TT)Fast for protecting semiconductors that fail quickly; slow for tolerating legitimate inrush from motors/transformers/capacitor banksUsing a slow-blow fuse to protect fast semiconductor loads — the silicon can fail before the fuse ever opens
The one habit that prevents most field failures For every protection or rectification component, explicitly write down the worst case you're designing against — not the typical case. Worst-case reverse voltage, worst-case surge current, worst-case ambient temperature, worst-case tolerance stack-up. Almost every real-world failure in this component family traces back to a rating that was checked against a nominal or typical condition instead of the actual worst case the part will see in the field.

Quick pre-schematic checklist

Diode / Schottky

  • Is V_RRM comfortably above the max reverse voltage the diode will see, including transients?
  • Does I_F(AV) cover continuous current with margin, and does I_FSM cover worst-case inrush?
  • Does switching speed (t_rr) matter at your operating frequency — if so, is Schottky justified over PN, and is the leakage/temperature trade-off acceptable?

TVS / GDT

  • Is V_RWM ≥ the max normal (not just nominal) line voltage, including charging/ripple peaks?
  • Is V_C at your expected I_PP safely below the protected IC's absolute max rating?
  • Uni or bidirectional — does the line ever go negative in normal operation?
  • If large surges are expected (lightning, power-cross), is a staged GDT+series-impedance+TVS needed?

Zener

  • Is the series resistor sized for worst-case I_Z at both minimum and maximum load current?
  • Does V_Z × I_Z stay under the package power rating at max current?
  • If it's a reference, are you in the 5.6–6.2 V near-zero-tempco band, or do you need temperature compensation?

Thermistor / Fuse

  • PTC vs fuse vs both — does the application need auto-reset, or is a one-time protective device acceptable (or preferred, for tamper-evidence)?
  • Does the fuse's I²t rating tolerate your circuit's normal inrush without nuisance blowing?
  • Is the fuse's voltage/breaking-capacity rating adequate for where it sits in the circuit, not just its current rating?
