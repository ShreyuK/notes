01

Transistors (BJT)

Bipolar Junction Transistors — basics, operating modes, and characteristic curves

What is a BJT?

A BJT is a three-terminal, current-controlled device. It's called "bipolar" because both electron and hole carriers participate in conduction — unlike a MOSFET, which relies on only one carrier type. It's built from three alternating-doped semiconductor layers.

Definition

Terminals: Base (B) · Collector (C) · Emitter (E)

Key rule: I_C = β × I_B

β (hFE) typically ranges 50–500, and varies significantly between individual devices of the same part number, and with temperature.

NPN Structure
N — Collector C
P — Base (thin) B
N — Emitter E

The base is deliberately thin and lightly doped so most carriers injected from the emitter sweep across into the collector rather than recombining.

The Four Rules (NPN)

  1. Collector must stay more positive than emitter for active-mode operation.
  2. The base–emitter junction behaves like a forward diode — roughly 0.6 V to turn on.
  3. Never exceed the datasheet's I_C, I_B, or V_CE limits.
  4. Never design a circuit around a specific β value — it varies 2–3× between individual parts and drifts with temperature. Good BJT design (e.g. emitter degeneration) makes behaviour insensitive to β.

Three Operating Regions

CUT-OFF

I_B = 0 · I_C ≈ 0

Both junctions reverse-biased.

Behaves like an open switch.

ACTIVE

I_C = β · I_B

Base–emitter forward, base–collector reverse.

Used for amplification.

SATURATION

V_CE ≈ 0.2 V

Both junctions forward-biased.

Behaves like a closed switch.

BJT Output Characteristics — I_C vs V_CE

I_C (mA) vs V_CE (V) for different base currents
Each curve = a fixed I_B. Watch the steep left edge vs the flat plateau.
Reading the curves
  • Steep left edge = saturation (V_CE < 0.3 V, fully ON) — a tiny ΔV_CE causes a huge ΔI_C.
  • Flat plateau = active region (I_C = β·I_B, amplifier mode) — I_C barely depends on V_CE here.
Key observations
  • I_C is set by I_B, not V_CE — current-source behaviour.
  • Slight upward slope in the "flat" region = Early Effect (base-width modulation).
  • I_B = 0 → cut-off region.
Why this matters

The flatness of the active-region plateau is exactly why BJTs make good amplifiers: I_C stays a clean, predictable multiple of I_B regardless of the voltage across the device. The Early Effect (slight slope) shows up in precision analog as a finite output resistance rather than an ideal current source.

MOSFETs — Structure, Family & Characteristic Curves

Metal-Oxide-Semiconductor Field-Effect Transistors

What is a MOSFET?

A MOSFET is a voltage-controlled device — dominant in digital and power electronics. Unlike the BJT, it's unipolar: only one carrier type conducts (electrons, for N-channel).

Definition & Terminals

Gate (G): control, zero DC current

Drain (D): output terminal

Source (S): reference terminal

N-Channel Structure
Gate (Metal / Poly-Si) G
SiO₂ Gate Oxide (insulator)
N+ Source     N+ Drain S
P-Body (channel forms at VGS > Vth)
N− Drift (sets voltage rating)
N+ Drain Substrate (heatsink tab) D

Current flows vertically: Drain (bottom) ↔ Source (top) through the channel.

Key advantages over BJT

Why MOSFETs dominate switching
  • Zero DC gate current (input resistance > 10¹⁴ Ω) — the gate is isolated by an oxide, not a PN junction.
  • No thermal runaway — R_DS(on) rises with temperature, so a hotter device carries less current. Self-balancing when paralleled.
  • Faster — no minority-carrier storage delay.
  • Easy to parallel — no ballast resistors needed (unlike BJTs).
  • I_D = κ(V_GS − V_th)² — quadratic law, vs BJT's linear I_C = β·I_B.
BJT vs MOSFET: thermal behaviour

A hotter BJT needs less V_BE for the same current — it hogs more current, heats further, and can run away. A hotter MOSFET has higher R_DS(on) — current is pushed toward cooler, lower-resistance devices instead. This is why paralleling MOSFETs is "safe by default," while paralleling BJTs needs emitter-degeneration resistors.

MOSFET Family Tree

N-Channel (Enhancement)
  • Electrons carry current → fast, low R_DS
  • V_GS positive (> V_th) to turn ON
  • Normally-OFF · ~95% of power designs
  • Examples: 2N7000, IRFZ44N, IRF540
P-Channel (Enhancement)
  • Holes carry current → higher R_DS, slower
  • V_GS negative (< −V_th) to turn ON
  • Used in high-side switches
  • Examples: IRF9540, FQP27P06
Depletion Mode (Normally-ON)
  • Channel exists at V_GS = 0
  • Negative V_GS turns it OFF
  • Used in current sources / limiters
  • Examples: DN2535, LND150
JFET vs MOSFET
  • JFET gate = PN junction (must stay reverse-biased, nA leakage)
  • MOSFET gate = SiO₂ insulated (zero DC current, ±20 V max, ESD-sensitive)

MOSFET Characteristic Curves

Transfer: I_D vs V_GS (V_th = 3 V)
I_D = 0 below V_th. Above it: I_D = κ(V_GS−V_th)² — quadratic rise.
Output: I_D (A) vs V_DS (V)
Steep rise = linear region (RDS(on)). Flat = saturation (amplifier).

MOSFETs by Material

Si · Super-Junction · SiC · GaN — and why bandgap is the whole story

Every row in this table is really a story about bandgap. A wider-bandgap material needs more energy to knock an electron from the valence band into the conduction band — so it tolerates a much higher electric field before avalanche breakdown. That means the drift region can be thinner and less resistive for the same voltage rating, directly lowering R_DS(on) and conduction loss.

Why GaN has no body diode

GaN HEMTs are lateral devices built on a fundamentally different structure (a 2D electron gas at the AlGaN/GaN interface) rather than a vertical P-N-P-N stack — there's no P-body/N-drain junction to form a parasitic diode. This eliminates reverse-recovery losses entirely, one of the reasons GaN chargers can switch in the MHz range.

Gate · Drain · Source Deep Dive

Terminals, depletion layer, body diode, and characteristic graphs

N-Channel MOSFET: terminals explained

Gate (G)
  • Insulated by SiO₂ — zero DC current
  • V_GS controls the channel → controls I_D
  • V_th typically 1–5 V
  • Never float · BV_GSS = ±20 V max
Drain (D)
  • Current enters here (N-ch)
  • Drain = back of die = heatsink tab
  • N− drift thickness sets BV_DSS
  • Inductive kick can spike V_DS!
Source (S)
  • Current exits here · V_GS reference
  • Low-side: Source = GND (easy drive)
  • High-side: needs bootstrap driver
  • Body tied to Source internally
Body Diode (intrinsic)
  • P-body → PN diode (S anode, D cathode)
  • Conducts when Drain is negative
  • V_f ≈ 0.7–1 V · slow t_rr 100–500 ns
  • Fix: Schottky in parallel, or use GaN
Never let the gate float

An unconnected, high-impedance gate can pick up static charge and drift to an unpredictable voltage — partially turning the device ON. Always use a pull-down (or pull-up) resistor on gate nodes.

Depletion layer & channel formation — step by step

How the N-channel turns ON:

STEP 1 · V_GS = 0 (OFF)

No gate voltage. P-body neutral.

No channel exists. Only nA leakage.

→ Cut-off

STEP 2 · 0 < V_GS < V_th

Gate attracts electrons, repels holes.

Depletion layer widens. Channel not yet formed.

→ Still OFF

STEP 3 · V_GS ≥ V_th (ON)

Surface inverts. Inversion layer forms.

Electrons flow S → D. I_D = κ(V_GS−V_th)²

→ Fully ON

Operating regions
  • Linear: V_DS < V_GS−V_th → acts as resistor R_DS(on)
  • Saturation: V_DS > V_GS−V_th → I_D constant
  • Breakdown: V_DS > BV_DSS → avalanche
Key equations
V_th = 1–5 V  (drops ~7 mV/°C)
I_D = κ(V_GS − V_th)²
R_DS(on) doubles from 25°C → 125°C
gm = 2κ(V_GS − V_th)

Gate-related graphs

R_DS(on) vs Gate Voltage V_GS
Why V_GS = 10 V?

R_DS(on) falls steeply then flattens. Drive V_GS ≥ 10 V (or ≥5 V for logic-level parts) for lowest loss.

Gate threshold V_th vs Temperature (°C)
Watch out

V_th drops ~7 mV/°C. A hot device turns on earlier — risk of partial turn-on with marginal gate drive.

Drain & source-related graphs

Capacitances (pF) vs Drain Voltage V_DS (V)
Gate–drain coupling

All capacitances drop as V_DS rises. C_rss (gate–drain Miller cap) causes the plateau in the gate charge curve.

Body diode: Source→Drain current vs V_SD (V)
Source–drain diode

Conducts when Source is positive vs Drain (~0.7 V knee). Useful for freewheeling, but slow recovery causes losses.

Gate charge & the Miller plateau

The single most important switching-behaviour graph for a power MOSFET. It shows how V_GS evolves as you push a controlled charge into the gate during turn-on.

Gate Voltage V_GS (V) vs Gate Charge Q_G (nC)
Three regions
  1. Region 1: C_iss charges up to V_th — nothing happens at the drain yet.
  2. Region 2 — Miller plateau: V_GS goes flat while V_DS collapses (governed by C_rss). This is the actual switching transition, and the slowest part.
  3. Region 3: V_GS rises the rest of the way to full drive, fully enhancing the channel.
Design rule

Gate drive current sets switching speed:

I_g = Q_G / t_switch

e.g. 100 nC in 10 ns → 10 A peak! Far beyond a microcontroller GPIO pin — use a proper gate driver IC.

Conduction & Switching Losses

Where the power goes in a MOSFET switch

Conduction losses

I²R heating while the MOSFET is fully ON.

Formula & example
P_cond = I_D² × R_DS(on) × D

Example: 10 A, 50 mΩ (25°C)
→ P = 100 × 0.05 = 5 W

At 125°C: R_DS ≈ 125 mΩ → 12.5 W!

How to reduce it
  1. Lower-R_DS(on) part
  2. Parallel MOSFETs (self-balancing)
  3. Full gate drive (V_GS = 10–15 V)
  4. SiC above 600 V
R_DS(on) multiplier vs junction temperature (°C)
Self-reinforcing loop

Since R_DS(on) rises with temperature, and conduction loss depends on R_DS(on), a poorly-cooled device can enter a loop: more current → more heat → higher resistance → more heat. This is why thermal design and R_DS(on) headroom both matter, even though it isn't the runaway BJTs experience.

Switching losses

Power dissipated during ON↔OFF transitions — while V_DS and I_D briefly overlap in time.

Turn-ON loss (E_on)

V_DS falls while I_D rises → overlap

E_on ≈ ½ × V_DS × I_D × t_on

Miller effect & Q_rr slow it down

Turn-OFF loss (E_off)

V_DS rises while I_D falls → overlap

E_off ≈ ½ × V_DS × I_D × t_off

Faster gate driver = less loss

Total loss (W) vs switching frequency — 10 A, 48 V example
Total loss
P = P_cond + ½·V_DS·I_D·(t_on+t_off)·f_sw + Q_G·V_GS·f_sw

Switching loss grows LINEARLY with f_sw.

How to reduce it

Low-Q_G part · strong gate driver · low C_rss · Schottky for Q_rr · ZVS/ZCS topologies · GaN for MHz operation.

The core design tension

At low frequency (10 kHz), conduction loss dominates total dissipation. By 500 kHz, switching loss has become dominant by far — sometimes by 5–6×. Raising switching frequency shrinks inductors, transformers, and capacitors, but only pays off if paired with a device/driver combination (low C_rss, fast driver, or GaN) that keeps t_on/t_off short.

When to use which?

Use BJT when...
  • Low-power amplifiers
  • Precision analog (gm = I_C/V_T)
  • Darlington high gain
  • Ultra-low cost
Use MOSFET when...
  • All digital logic
  • Power switching < 400 V
  • High frequency (>100 kHz)
  • Paralleling for current
Use IGBT when...
  • High voltage > 400 V
  • Medium freq (<50 kHz)
  • EV / solar inverters
  • Industrial motor drives

07

Key Takeaways

The five things to walk away with
1
BJT

Current-controlled (I_C = β·I_B). Cut-off / active / saturation. Never design around β.

2
MOSFET

Voltage-controlled, zero gate current. V_GS > V_th forms the inversion channel.

3
Terminals

Gate: insulated, ±20 V max. Drain: sets BV_DSS, heatsink tab. Source: reference. Body diode: slow but useful.

4
Losses

P_cond = I_D²·R_DS(on) (doubles when hot). P_sw ∝ f_sw. Use the charts to pick the right device.

5
Choice

MOSFET: speed, digital, <400 V. BJT: precision analog, cost. IGBT: >400 V power.
