High-Speed Board Layout, From Scratch

A ground-up walkthrough of Altera's AN-224 application note — built for someone who knows digital systems and firmware, but is new to PCB layout and signal integrity.

00Why this matters

When you write firmware, a signal is either a 1 or a 0 — clean, instant, abstract. On a real circuit board, a signal is a voltage traveling down a physical piece of copper, and copper doesn't care about your abstractions. It has resistance, it has capacitance, it has inductance, and at the switching speeds modern FPGAs and processors use (edges under 100 picoseconds), a trace on a PCB stops behaving like "a wire" and starts behaving like a miniature radio transmission line.

This document (Altera AN-224) is about keeping that physical reality from corrupting your logical 1s and 0s. It covers five goals that show up over and over in high-speed design:

  • Clean, evenly distributed power — every chip needs stable voltage, even while other pins on the same chip are switching violently.
  • Termination — matching impedance so a signal doesn't bounce back and forth like an echo.
  • Minimizing crosstalk — stopping one trace's signal from bleeding into its neighbor.
  • Reducing ground bounce — controlling the noise created when many outputs switch at once.
  • Matching impedance — making sure the "electrical highway" a signal travels on has a consistent width the whole way.
Beginner note

Everything in this guide ultimately serves one idea: a fast digital edge behaves like an RF signal, and PCB traces, power planes, and connectors are the antennas, waveguides, and transmission lines it travels through. Once you accept that framing, every rule in this document — "keep stubs short," "route traces close to a ground plane," "add decoupling capacitors" — becomes a specific consequence of that one physical fact.

01Electronics primer: the concepts you need first

Before the document's own content, here are the concepts it assumes you already have. If you're comfortable with these, skip ahead to Material Selection.

What is impedance, really?

In DC circuits you only deal with resistance (R) — opposition to current that doesn't change with time. But a fast-switching signal is not DC; it's constantly changing voltage, which means it also interacts with two other properties:

  • Capacitance — the ability of two conductors (like a trace and a ground plane) to store charge between them. More capacitance resists fast changes in voltage.
  • Inductance — the property of a conductor that resists fast changes in current, caused by the magnetic field a moving charge creates.

Impedance is the combined opposition a signal feels from all three (R, L, and C) at a given frequency. A PCB trace running over a ground plane has a specific, calculable impedance called its characteristic impedance (Z₀) — essentially "how hard is it for a changing signal to push current down this trace." This is a property of the trace's geometry and the board material, not of what's connected to it.

Why do mismatched impedances cause reflections?

Why it happens

Picture a signal as a wave traveling down a garden hose full of water. If the hose suddenly narrows or widens, some of that wave's energy bounces backward instead of continuing forward — because the "impedance" of the pipe changed. Electrically, the same thing happens at any point where Z₀ changes: at a via, a connector, a stub, or the receiving pin itself. Some of the signal's energy reflects back toward the source instead of being absorbed by the receiver.

If the source impedance, trace impedance, and load impedance are all matched, the signal is absorbed cleanly with no reflection — like a wave hitting a wall of the exact same material it's traveling through. If they don't match, reflections travel back and forth, adding ripples (ringing) on top of your clean square wave. Enough ringing can cross the receiver's switching threshold at the wrong time and register as a false logic transition.

What is a "trace" electrically?

A copper trace is never just a wire — it always has a distributed mix of resistance, inductance, and capacitance along its length, especially relative to whatever conductor (ground or power plane) sits near it. This is why the document calls a trace a "transmission line": it's not a single lumped component, it's a continuous structure with electrical properties that depend on its width, thickness, height above a reference plane, and the material around it.

Rise time and why it matters more than clock frequency

A common beginner assumption is that "high speed" means "high clock frequency." It's actually more about edge rate (rise/fall time) — how fast a signal transitions from low to high. A signal can have a modest clock frequency but an extremely fast edge, and it's the edge speed that determines how much a trace behaves like a transmission line versus a simple wire. Sub-100-picosecond edges (as in modern FPGA I/O) put even fairly short traces into transmission-line territory.

02Material selection

The insulating material between copper layers on a PCB — the dielectric — isn't a passive bystander. It directly changes how signals propagate.

Dielectric constant (εr)

Every insulating material has a property called permittivity (ε), which describes how it responds to an electric field. The dielectric constant is just that material's permittivity compared to a vacuum's:

Equation 2 — Dielectric constant
εr = ε / ε₀
ε₀ = permittivity of empty space. εr tells you how much a material amplifies capacitance between two conductors compared to vacuum/air.

Why do you care? Because εr directly affects a trace's impedance and how fast a signal propagates through it: higher εr → slower signal, and it changes the impedance equations you'll see in the next section.

Loss tangent — why it matters on long traces

The loss tangent describes how much of a signal's high-frequency energy the dielectric material absorbs and converts to heat as the signal travels (this is called dielectric absorption). A higher loss tangent attenuates high frequencies more aggressively over distance. Since a fast digital edge is made up of many high-frequency components (more on this in the Pre-Emphasis section), a lossy dielectric on a long trace will "round off" a sharp edge into a slow, blurry one — degrading signal quality even if impedance is perfectly matched.

Beginner note

FR-4 is the default, inexpensive material used on the vast majority of PCBs — including almost every hobbyist board you've probably already handled. GETEK and other lower-loss materials exist specifically for very high-speed/long-trace designs where FR-4's absorption becomes a real problem. For most of your projects, FR-4 is fine; this becomes relevant once you're pushing multi-Gbps serial links over long boards.

03Transmission lines: microstrip vs. stripline

Every trace on a PCB falls into one of two categories, based on where it sits relative to reference (ground/power) planes:

A microstrip trace runs on the top or bottom (outer) layer with one reference plane below it. A stripline trace is buried on an inner layer, sandwiched between two reference planes.

Both share the same three geometric variables that determine impedance: W (trace width), T (trace thickness, often given in ounces of copper — 1 oz ≈ 1.4 mil), and H (height between the trace and its reference plane).

The impedance equations

Equation 3 — Microstrip impedance
Z₀ = [87 / √(εr+1.41)] · ln( 5.98H / (0.8W+T) ) Ω
Units in mils. Example: W=8, H=5, T=1.4, εr=4.1 → Z₀ ≈ 50 Ω, the standard single-ended target impedance.
Equation 4 — Stripline impedance
Z₀ = [60 / √εr] · ln( 4H / (0.67(T+0.8W)) ) Ω
Example: W=9, H=24, T=1.4, εr=4.1 → Z₀ ≈ 50 Ω. Note stripline needs a much larger H for the same Z₀ — see below.

You will basically never solve these by hand in real work (SI tools do it), but internalizing the relationships is what actually matters:

  • Impedance goes up as trace width goes down (a thinner trace = less capacitive coupling to ground = higher impedance).
  • Impedance goes up as the trace sits farther from its reference plane (H increases).
  • Impedance goes down as trace thickness increases.
  • A stripline needs roughly 3-5× the dielectric height of a microstrip to hit the same impedance, because being surrounded by dielectric on both sides raises the effective εr the trace "feels" (no exposure to air, which has εr≈1). This is why stripline-heavy boards are physically thicker.

Why is 50 Ω the magic number?

50 Ω isn't fundamental physics — it's an industry convention, chosen decades ago as a practical compromise for coaxial cable (it balances power-handling and low-loss characteristics) and it stuck as the standard for single-ended digital I/O. 100 Ω differential is the equivalent convention for differential pairs (LVDS, etc.). Your job as a layout designer is to hit whatever target impedance the I/O standard and the driving chip's datasheet specify — often 50 Ω single-ended or 100 Ω differential — consistently along the entire trace length.

04Propagation delay

Signals don't travel down a trace at the speed of light in a vacuum — the dielectric slows them down. Propagation delay (tPD) is simply how long it takes a signal edge to travel a given trace length, and it's directly tied to εr:

Equations 5 & 6 — Propagation delay
tPD(microstrip) = 0.475·εr + 0.67   |   tPD(stripline) = 0.85·√εr
Result in picoseconds per inch. Higher εr → slower propagation. Stripline is slightly slower than microstrip at the same εr because it's fully surrounded by dielectric rather than partly exposed to air.
Why it matters

Propagation delay is why trace length matching is a real discipline in high-speed design (you'll see it referenced constantly for DDR memory, differential pairs, and clock trees). Two signals that need to arrive at a receiver at the same time — say, the two halves of a differential pair, or multiple data lines that share one clock — must have matched electrical lengths, not just matched physical lengths, since εr variation between layers or trace types can offset that.

05Pre-emphasis

This section explains a distortion problem and a clever fix for it.

The problem: traces act as low-pass filters

A trace (plus the dielectric it runs through) doesn't transmit all frequencies equally — it attenuates high frequencies more than low frequencies, which is exactly what a low-pass filter does. Here's the key insight: a sharp square-wave digital edge is mathematically made up of a huge stack of sine waves at many different frequencies (this is Fourier analysis). The sharp transition edges are built from high-frequency components; the flat top/bottom regions are built from low-frequency components.

So when a square wave travels down a lossy trace, its high-frequency content (the edges) gets attenuated more than its low-frequency content (the flat parts). The practical result: the edge slows down and rounds off, rise time increases, and the "eye" (the open space in an eye diagram where a receiver reliably distinguishes 1 from 0) shrinks — raising the chance of bit errors.

The fix: pre-emphasis

Pre-emphasis deliberately boosts the signal's amplitude right at the transition regions before it enters the lossy trace — overshooting on purpose. Since the trace will attenuate those high frequencies anyway, by the time the boosted edge reaches the receiver, it has been "corrected" back down to a clean, sharp transition. It's the electrical equivalent of shouting the beginning of a word so it's still audible after an echo-y hallway swallows part of it.

Beginner note

Stratix GX (the transceiver-capable FPGA family this note targets) exposes pre-emphasis as a configurable percentage (5–25%) applied on top of the output differential voltage — this lets you tune the boost to match how lossy your specific trace/cable is. Modern SerDes standards (PCIe, USB, etc.) all use variations of this same technique, often paired with the receiver-side equivalent, "equalization."

06Crosstalk and routing scheme

Crosstalk is unwanted energy coupling from one trace into a neighboring trace — through the capacitance and inductance they share due to proximity. A switching "aggressor" trace induces a small unwanted signal on a nearby quiet "victim" trace.

Core rules to reduce it

  • Widen spacing between parallel signal lines as much as routing allows — avoid bringing traces closer than 3× the dielectric height (H) to each other.
  • Route the trace as close to its reference (ground) plane as possible — this couples it tightly to ground and "starves" the coupling path to its neighbors.
  • Use differential routing for critical nets, keeping both traces' lengths and geometry matched.
  • If two single-ended signals must run close together, route them on different layers, oriented orthogonally (90° to each other) rather than parallel.
  • Minimize how long any two traces run parallel and coupled — short parallel runs couple far less energy than long ones.
Rule of thumb: keep at least 4× the trace width (A) between the centers of two adjacent parallel single-ended traces. Reducing the height above the ground plane (H) also helps, without needing to change this spacing.

Dual-stripline layouts need extra care

Some boards stack two signal layers directly adjacent to each other (a "dual-stripline" arrangement) to save layer count. Because both layers are far from a reference plane relative to each other, they couple more strongly. To manage this: route the two layers' traces perpendicular to each other, increase the separation between the two signal layers, and minimize the distance from each signal layer to its nearest reference plane.

07Clock routing schemes

Clocks are especially sensitive to signal integrity problems because any distortion (ringing, jitter) directly translates to timing uncertainty across your whole synchronous design. Four routing topologies show up repeatedly when one clock source must reach multiple devices:

Daisy chain — with stubs

The main clock trace runs past each device, and a short stub branches off to reach each device's pin. Simple to route, but every stub is a small impedance discontinuity that reflects part of the signal back onto the main bus.

Daisy chain — without stubs

The main bus is routed directly through each device's pin in sequence — no branch, no stub. This removes the impedance mismatch a stub creates entirely, at the cost of more constrained routing (the trace must physically enter and exit each device's package footprint).

Star routing

Every device gets its own dedicated trace running directly from the clock source, like spokes on a wheel. All traces must be length- and load-matched to avoid clock skew between devices, and the source's output impedance must match the combined load of all branches.

Serpentine routing

When traces to different devices would naturally end up different lengths, you deliberately add "wiggles" (meanders) to the shorter ones so all traces end up electrically equal length. Altera actually recommends avoiding this where possible — sharp bends packed close together can themselves cause crosstalk between the parallel segments of the same trace — and suggests smooth arcs instead of tight meanders when length-matching is unavoidable.

Beginner note

General clock-routing hygiene, regardless of topology: keep traces as straight as possible (avoid sharp 90° bends — use gentle arcs), never split a clock across multiple layers, avoid vias on clock lines (each via is an impedance discontinuity), and always terminate clock lines properly (see the Termination section).

08Why stub length matters so much

A "stub" is a short trace segment branching off the main signal path (as in daisy-chain routing above, or between a termination resistor and a receiver pin). The document gives a concrete rule for how long is "too long":

Stub length limit
TDstub < (T10%-90%) / 3
TDstub = electrical delay of the stub; T10%-90% = the signal's rise/fall time. For a 1ns rise time, this works out to roughly 0.5 inches of maximum stub length.

Why a third of the rise time? If a stub's round-trip delay is a significant fraction of the edge's rise time, the reflection bouncing off the stub's open end arrives back at the main trace while the edge is still transitioning — distorting the very edge that caused it. Shorten the stub enough, and that reflection returns and settles well before the edge finishes, so it never has a chance to interfere.

09Differential pair routing

A differential pair sends the same signal twice — once inverted — down two matched traces. The receiver looks at the difference between them rather than either trace's absolute voltage relative to ground. This makes differential signaling far more resistant to noise: any noise picked up equally by both traces (common-mode noise) cancels out at the receiver, since only the difference matters.

A differential pair: two matched-width (W) traces separated by a fixed gap (S), routed together over a reference plane at height H.

Key rules for routing them well:

  • Keep the spacing (S) between the two traces of a pair constant over their entire length — varying it changes the pair's differential impedance partway through, causing reflections.
  • Keep both traces of a pair the same length — any length mismatch (skew) reintroduces a timing offset, which shows up as common-mode noise and reduces the immunity the pair was supposed to provide.
  • Set S ≈ 3H as the pair leaves the driving device, to minimize reflection.
  • If you have two separate differential pairs near each other, keep the distance between pairs (D) greater than 2× the intra-pair spacing (D > 2S) to limit crosstalk between the pairs themselves.
  • Avoid unnecessary vias — each one is a small impedance bump and adds extra inductance.

10Termination schemes

Termination means intentionally placing a resistor (or resistor network) so that the impedance the signal "sees" at the end of the line matches the trace's characteristic impedance Z₀ — eliminating reflections at that point. Different schemes trade off power consumption, board space, and signal quality differently.

Simple parallel

One resistor (RT = Z₀) placed at the receiver, tied between the signal and ground. Straightforward and effective, but it constantly draws DC current whenever the line is held high — burning power continuously.

Thévenin (voltage-divider) parallel

Splits the termination resistor into two (R1, R2) between VCC and GND, sized so their parallel combination still equals Z₀. Reduces current drawn from the driving chip, but now constantly draws current from the power supply instead — you've moved the power cost, not eliminated it.

Active parallel

Like simple parallel, but the resistor ties to a separate bias voltage (VBIAS) chosen so the driver can both source and sink current symmetrically. Needs an extra voltage supply capable of sinking and sourcing current — more complex, but more power-efficient than a straight pull to ground.

Series-RC ("AC") parallel

A resistor and capacitor in series form the termination. The capacitor blocks DC current entirely (no constant power draw!) while still letting the resistor absorb high-frequency reflections. Trade-off: the capacitor must be sized carefully — too small and it stops filtering effectively, too large and it starts delaying the signal. Requires roughly balanced high/low time in the signal.

Series termination

Instead of terminating at the load, you place a resistor right at the source, sized so RT + the driver's own output impedance equals Z₀. Very power-efficient (no DC path to ground at all), but it slows the rise time somewhat due to the added RC time constant — and it fundamentally only works well for a single receiver at the end of the line, not multiple taps along it.

Differential pair termination

A single resistor bridges the two traces of a differential pair right at the receiver, sized to match the pair's differential impedance (typically 100 Ω for LVDS/LVPECL). Some Altera device families (Stratix GX) can implement this on-chip, eliminating the external resistor and the board space/stub length it would otherwise need.

Beginner note

Notice the recurring "fly-by" variant mentioned for several schemes — this just means placing the termination resistor at the far end of a long trace (past the receiver's stub) rather than right at the receiver pin, which avoids creating yet another stub between the resistor and the pin when the termination line itself needs to be long.

11Simultaneous switching noise & ground bounce

When many output pins on the same chip switch at the same instant, they all momentarily draw a burst of current through the chip's shared ground and power connections (bond wires, package pins, vias). That shared path has some inductance, and a sudden current change through inductance creates a voltage spike — this is ground bounce (the chip's internal "ground" reference momentarily shifts relative to the board's actual ground) and its counterpart on the power side, VCC sag.

Why it happens

Recall from the primer: inductance resists sudden changes in current, and the voltage it generates while doing so is proportional to how fast that current is changing (V = L·di/dt). More simultaneously switching outputs, or faster switching, means a bigger di/dt, and therefore a bigger unwanted voltage spike on a pin every other signal on that chip is referenced against.

Practical mitigations from the document

  • Configure unused I/O as outputs driven low (acts as an extra "virtual ground") or high (to counter VCC sag), rather than leaving them floating.
  • Interleave ground pins between switching I/O pins wherever the package allows.
  • Reduce how many outputs can switch simultaneously, and spread the ones that must switch evenly across the device rather than clustering them.
  • Use the slow slew-rate output option when the extra speed isn't needed — a slower edge means a smaller di/dt.
  • Add 0.01–0.1 µF decoupling capacitors near power pins (plus a 0.001 µF cap for filtering noise above 100 MHz), placed as physically close to the VCC/GND pin pair as possible, connected with short, wide traces and generously sized vias to minimize the inductance in that connection path.
  • Connect each ground pin/via to the ground plane individually rather than daisy-chaining several pins to one via — daisy-chaining shares (and lengthens) the return-current path, adding inductance right where you're trying to remove it.
Ideal decoupling capacitor placement: via touching (or nearly touching) the capacitor pad, connected with the widest, shortest trace practical — every extra millimeter of trace adds inductance that undermines the capacitor's high-frequency filtering.

12Power filtering and distribution

Filtering — removing noise from the supply itself

Power arriving from your regulator or supply isn't perfectly clean DC — it carries low-frequency ripple and, once distributed across a busy board, picks up high-frequency noise from switching devices. Two complementary techniques handle these:

  • Bulk capacitors (e.g. 100 µF electrolytic) placed where power enters the board or right after a regulator, filtering low-frequency (<1 kHz) noise and supplying the burst current needed when many outputs switch at once.
  • Ferrite beads in series with the supply, paired with a bypass capacitor after the bead. A ferrite bead acts almost like a short circuit at low frequencies but presents significant impedance at high frequencies — so it blocks high-frequency noise from traveling further down the power path, while the capacitor after it shunts any remaining low-frequency noise to ground.
  • Small decoupling capacitors right at each chip's VCC/GND pins, handling the highest-frequency noise generated locally by that chip's own switching.

Distribution — getting power to every device evenly

Two approaches:

  • Power planes — a full copper layer dedicated to VCC (and another to GND) on a multi-layer board. Because the plane covers the entire board area, its DC resistance is extremely low, so voltage stays uniform everywhere and it can supply large bursts of current without sagging. This is the recommended approach.
  • Power bus (trace) networks — wide traces carrying VCC/GND, typically on cheaper 2-layer boards where full planes aren't available. Cheaper, but has meaningfully higher DC resistance, meaning voltage can sag more across the board and current-sink capability is lower.

For mixed analog/digital designs (e.g. a board with an FPGA's PLL, which is sensitive to power noise), it's worth using separate power planes for analog and digital circuitry, or at minimum split planes — physically separated "islands" of one plane layer — with a generous gap (25-100 mil) between the analog and digital regions, joined at a single common ground point to avoid digital switching noise bleeding into the sensitive analog supply.

13Electromagnetic interference (EMI)

Any changing current or voltage radiates some electromagnetic energy — this is simply a physical fact of moving charge, and every real PCB does it to some degree. EMI matters because excessive radiated or conducted noise can violate regulatory limits and interfere with other circuits (including your own).

EMI is proportional to both the rate of change of a signal (fast edges radiate more) and the series inductance in its current path (which is exactly what a wide, short trace close to a reference plane minimizes). Practical mitigations largely repeat themes from earlier sections, because they're the same underlying physics:

  • Sandwich each signal layer between a ground and a power plane where your layer count allows — this is the "quietest" possible position for a trace, since return current has a short, direct path on both sides.
  • Keep ground planes physically close to signal layers to minimize inductance in the return path.
  • Choose low-ESR, low-ESL surface-mount components, particularly capacitors.
  • Always give return current the shortest possible path back to its source (this is really the same rule as "route close to a reference plane," restated from the return-current side).
An example 8-layer stack-up (per the document): stripline signal layers sandwiched by ground planes are the electrically "quietest" — every signal layer has a nearby, low-inductance return path on both sides.

14FPGA-specific: configuration, JTAG, test points

DCLK (configuration clock)

During device configuration (loading the FPGA's bitstream via passive serial/parallel modes), the DCLK signal drives edge-triggered configuration logic. Because it's edge-triggered, it's just as sensitive to overshoot, ringing, and crosstalk as any other clock — route it using the same clock-routing guidelines covered earlier. If more than five configuration devices share one DCLK, use a buffer to split the fan-out rather than driving them all directly from one source.

JTAG

JTAG (IEEE 1149.1) is the standard boundary-scan and in-system-programming interface, using four required signals — TDI, TDO, TMS, TCK — plus an optional TRST reset. As boards get denser, traditional "bed-of-nails" physical test probing becomes impractical, making JTAG's ability to test connectivity and program devices through a serial chain increasingly essential rather than optional. Route TCK using clock-routing rules, and for long JTAG chains, use multiple shorter chains and minimize the trace length between one device's TDO and the next device's TDI to reduce accumulated delay.

Test points

With BGA packages mounted on the board, you often can't physically probe a pin directly — and doing so risks damaging the device. A dedicated via connected to the signal under test (with as short a stub as possible) provides a safe, planned probe point instead. Note the tension here: the via itself is an impedance discontinuity, so even a "test point" has to be designed with the same signal-integrity care as any other stub.

15Glossary

Characteristic impedance (Z₀)
The impedance a signal "sees" traveling down a trace of a given geometry over a reference plane — a property of the trace itself, independent of what's connected to either end.
Microstrip
A trace on an outer PCB layer with one reference plane below it.
Stripline
A trace on an inner PCB layer, sandwiched between two reference planes.
Reflection / ringing
Signal energy bouncing backward at an impedance mismatch, adding unwanted ripple on top of the intended waveform.
Crosstalk
Unwanted signal coupling from one trace (aggressor) into a nearby trace (victim) via shared capacitance/inductance.
Stub
A short branch trace off a main signal path; long stubs create reflections proportional to how large a fraction of the signal's rise time their round-trip delay consumes.
Termination
A resistor (or RC network) placed to make the impedance at a trace's end match Z₀, absorbing the signal instead of reflecting it.
Ground bounce / VCC sag
Voltage spikes on a chip's internal ground/power reference caused by many outputs switching simultaneously through shared, inductive package connections.
Decoupling capacitor
A small capacitor placed close to a chip's power pins to supply instantaneous switching current locally and filter high-frequency power noise.
Loss tangent
A material property describing how much signal energy a dielectric absorbs (converts to heat) at a given frequency; higher values attenuate high frequencies more over distance.
Pre-emphasis
Deliberately boosting a signal's amplitude at transition edges before it enters a lossy channel, so the channel's attenuation restores a clean edge by the time it reaches the receiver.
Differential pair
Two matched, closely-spaced traces carrying inverted copies of the same signal; the receiver reads only their difference, canceling common-mode noise.
EMI
Electromagnetic interference — energy unintentionally radiated or conducted by changing currents/voltages, proportional to edge rate and series inductance.
