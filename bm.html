01BJT Fundamentals

Bipolar Junction Transistor — the current-controlled original

What it is

A three-terminal, current-controlled device built from two back-to-back PN junctions (NPN or PNP). Terminals: Base (B), Collector (C), Emitter (E). A small base current controls a much larger collector current.

I_C = β · I_B   (β = h_FE, typically 50–500)

Four rules that never change (NPN):

  • Collector must sit more positive than the Emitter
  • Base–Emitter junction is a forward diode (~0.6–0.7 V ON)
  • Never exceed the I_C, I_B, or V_CE absolute maximums
  • Never design a circuit that depends on an exact β value — it varies 3–5× across the same part number and with temperature

The three operating regions

RegionConditionBehaviour
Cut-offI_B = 0Open switch, I_C ≈ 0
Active0 < V_BE(on), V_CE > V_CE(sat)Amplifier: I_C = β·I_B
SaturationV_CE ≈ 0.2 VClosed switch, fully ON

On the I_C–V_CE output-curve family (I_C vs V_CE for a family of I_B steps): the steep left edge is saturation, the flat plateau is the active region where I_C is set by I_B (current-source behaviour), and a slight upward slope across the flat region is the Early effect (finite output resistance). I_B = 0 collapses the whole family to the cut-off line.

Why this matters on a schematic
Because I_C tracks I_B and β drifts with temperature and from unit to unit, a BJT switch design must saturate hard (drive I_B well beyond I_C/β_min) rather than relying on a specific gain — that's rule 4 above, and it's the single most common BJT design mistake.

02MOSFET Fundamentals

Metal-Oxide-Semiconductor FET — the voltage-controlled workhorse of power electronics

Definition & terminals

A voltage-controlled transistor, dominant in digital logic and power switching.

  • Gate (G) — control terminal, insulated by SiO₂, draws essentially zero DC current
  • Drain (D) — output/power terminal, usually tied to the metal tab / heatsink in a TO-220
  • Source (S) — reference terminal for V_GS
I_D = κ(V_GS − V_th)²  (above threshold, saturation region)

Why MOSFETs replaced BJTs in most designs

  • Zero DC gate current (input R > 10¹⁴ Ω) → almost no drive power needed once switched
  • No thermal runaway — R_DS(on) rises with temperature, so current self-limits (this is what makes direct paralleling safe, unlike BJTs)
  • Faster — majority-carrier device, no minority-carrier storage time
  • Easy to parallel for higher current

Reading the characteristic curves

Transfer curve (I_D vs V_GS): I_D = 0 below V_th (device OFF). Above V_th it rises with a quadratic shape — this is the square-law region used for analog/linear operation.
Output curves (I_D vs V_DS, family of V_GS): the steep rising portion is the linear/triode region (behaves like a voltage-controlled resistor — this is where a power MOSFET switch operates, sized for the lowest possible R_DS(on)). The flat portion is the saturation region (constant-current, amplifier-style operation). Higher V_GS pushes more current before flattening out.

03MOSFET Family Tree

Channel type and depletion vs enhancement mode

N-Channel (Enhancement)

  • Electrons carry current → fast, low R_DS(on)
  • V_GS must be positive (> V_th) to turn ON
  • Normally-OFF — used in ~95% of power designs
  • Examples: 2N7000, IRFZ44N, IRF540

P-Channel (Enhancement)

  • Holes carry current → higher R_DS(on), slower
  • V_GS must be negative (< −V_th) to turn ON
  • Used mainly in high-side switches (simpler gate drive vs N-ch high-side)
  • Examples: IRF9540, FQP27P06

Depletion Mode (Normally-ON)

  • Channel already exists at V_GS = 0
  • A negative V_GS is required to pinch it off
  • Used in current sources / current limiters
  • Examples: DN2535, LND150

JFET vs MOSFET

  • JFET gate = PN junction — must stay reverse-biased, nA-level leakage
  • MOSFET gate = SiO₂ insulated — zero DC gate current, but ±20 V absolute max and ESD-sensitive (a static discharge can punch through the oxide permanently)

04MOSFETs by Semiconductor Material

Same device physics, different substrate — this is what actually sets voltage, speed, and cost

Silicon (Si)

  • 1.1 eV bandgap · T_j 150 °C · ≤ 650 V
  • Cheapest, most mature process
  • Use: < 200 V supplies, motor drives
  • IRFZ44N, IRF540N

Super-Junction Si

  • P/N pillar structure breaks the normal R_DS(on)-vs-voltage tradeoff
  • 500–900 V range
  • Use: PFC stages, offline SMPS
  • CoolMOS, MDmesh

Silicon Carbide (SiC)

  • 3.3 eV bandgap · T_j 200 °C · 650–4500 V
  • Best above 600 V — much lower switching loss than Si at high voltage
  • Use: EV traction, solar inverters
  • Wolfspeed C3M

GaN HEMT

  • 3.4 eV bandgap · 40–650 V · MHz+ switching
  • Lowest switching losses of any category — no body diode to reverse-recover
  • GaN Systems, EPC

DMOS (Power Si)

  • Vertical structure — Drain is the die back / package tab
  • Has an intrinsic body diode (parasitic, from the P-body/N-drift junction)
  • Structure used in essentially all discrete power MOSFETs

IGBT (reference point)

  • MOS gate driving a BJT output stage — voltage-controlled input, bipolar-level conduction
  • 600–6500 V
  • Slower (tail current) but wins decisively above ~400 V
  • STGP10NC60

05Gate · Drain · Source Deep Dive

The parasitics that actually determine switching behaviour

Parasitic capacitances vs V_DS

C_iss, C_oss, C_rss — all three drop sharply as V_DS rises from zero and then flatten out at higher voltage.

  • C_iss= C_gs + C_gd. Input capacitance the gate driver has to charge every switching cycle.
  • C_oss= C_ds + C_gd. Output capacitance; stores energy every cycle (½·C_oss·V_DS² lost each transition) and rings with circuit inductance.
  • C_rss= C_gd, the "Miller" capacitance. Small in value but disproportionately important — see Gate Charge below.

    • Body (Source→Drain) diode

    Intrinsic to the vertical DMOS structure. Conducts when Source is positive with respect to Drain, with a forward knee around 0.7 V, and the forward current rises steeply (exponentially) beyond that knee — same shape as any silicon diode.

    Useful for freewheeling / synchronous-rectification duty, but it is slow: its reverse-recovery charge (Q_rr) causes real loss and shoot-through risk any time it's forced to turn off quickly (e.g. in a synchronous buck's low-side FET). GaN devices have no body diode at all, which is a major reason they switch so cleanly.

    06Gate Charge & the Miller Plateau

    The single most important curve for sizing a gate driver

    Gate charge curve — V_GS vs Q_G. This is measured, not calculated, and is on every power MOSFET datasheet.

    The three regions

    • Region 1 — C_iss charges up to V_th; drain current is still zero.
    • Region 2 — Miller plateau — V_GS goes flat while V_DS collapses from the rail down to R_DS(on)·I_D. All the gate current here is going into C_gd (C_rss), not raising V_GS. This is the region where switching loss actually happens, because V and I overlap.
    • Region 3 — V_GS rises the rest of the way to full drive voltage, pulling R_DS(on) down to its final low value.

    Design rule

    Gate drive current sets switching speed:

    I_g = Q_G / t_switch

    Example: 100 nC of total gate charge switched in 10 ns needs 10 A of peak gate drive current. A logic-level GPIO pin cannot do this — you need a proper gate driver IC (or discrete totem-pole stage) sized for that peak current, not just the DC logic level.

    07Conduction & Switching Losses

    Where the power actually goes in a MOSFET switch

    Conduction loss — I²R heating while fully ON

    P_cond = I_D² × R_DS(on) × D

    Example: 10 A through 50 mΩ at 25 °C → P = 100 × 0.05 = 5 W.
    At 125 °C junction temperature R_DS(on) roughly doubles to ~125 mΩ → 12.5 W for the same current. R_DS(on) has a strong positive temperature coefficient — always design against the hot value, never the 25 °C datasheet headline number.

    How to reduce it: lower-R_DS(on) part · parallel MOSFETs (safe because R_DS(on) self-balances with temperature) · full gate drive (V_GS = 10–15 V, not a marginal 4–5 V) · SiC above 600 V.

    Switching loss — power during ON↔OFF transitions

    E_on ≈ ½·V_DS·I_D·t_on E_off ≈ ½·V_DS·I_D·t_off

    Turn-on: V_DS falls while I_D rises → the overlap region dissipates energy; the Miller plateau and the body diode's reverse-recovery charge (Q_rr, from the diode being reverse-recovered by the complementary switch) both slow this down.

    Turn-off: V_DS rises while I_D falls → same overlap mechanism in reverse. A faster/stronger gate driver directly reduces this loss.

    How to reduce it: low-Q_G part · strong gate driver · low C_rss · Schottky diodes to cut reverse-recovery loss · ZVS/ZCS topologies · GaN for MHz-class designs.

    Total loss budget

    P_total = P_cond + ½·V_DS·I_D·(t_on + t_off)·f_sw + Q_G·V_GS·f_sw

    Switching loss grows linearly with switching frequency while conduction loss doesn't care about frequency at all — this is exactly why there's always a frequency where a design's total loss bottoms out, and why raising f_sw to shrink magnetics eventually costs you back in switch dissipation (a 10 A / 48 V example easily goes from ~5 W total at 10 kHz to ~30 W at 500 kHz on the same part).

    09Application Circuits

    Why each device is used, where, and the exact topology to reach for

    A · NPN BJT LOW-SIDE SWITCH (relay / inductive load)

    Why: BJT saturates hard at low cost for a simple ON/OFF load. R_B is sized so I_B ≥ I_C(load)/β_min with margin — never trust the "typical" β on the datasheet. The flyback diode is mandatory across any inductive load (relay, motor, solenoid) — without it, the collapsing magnetic field generates a voltage spike that will exceed V_CE(max) and destroy the transistor the instant it turns off.

    B · NPN COMMON-EMITTER AMPLIFIER (voltage-divider bias)

    Why: R1/R2 set a stable DC base voltage independent of β (a "stiff" divider), R_E adds negative feedback that further stabilizes the bias point against β and temperature drift, and C_E bypasses R_E at AC so the stage still gets full gain. This is the standard small-signal analog building block — used where a MOSFET's gate threshold spread would make biasing awkward.

    C · N-CHANNEL MOSFET LOW-SIDE SWITCH (with gate driver)

    Why each part is there: a dedicated gate driver IC (not a bare GPIO) supplies the amp-level peak current I_g = Q_G/t_switch needed to slew through the Miller plateau quickly. R_G in series damps ringing from the driver-to-gate loop inductance against C_iss. R_pd (pull-down) holds the gate firmly at 0 V whenever the driver output is high-impedance (e.g. at power-up) — an unterminated gate can float above V_th and turn the FET partially ON. The flyback diode is required for the same reason as the BJT case.

    D · SYNCHRONOUS HALF-BRIDGE (bootstrap high-side drive)

    Why this topology: the high-side N-channel FET's source sits on the switching node, so its gate must be driven above V_BUS — the bootstrap capacitor is charged toward V_CC through the boot diode whenever the switch node is low, then floats up with the source to supply the high-side driver once the switch node goes high. A P-channel high-side switch avoids this complexity (gate only needs to swing to ground) at the cost of higher R_DS(on) and slower switching, which is why N-ch + bootstrap wins for anything above a few amps or above ~100 kHz.

    Dead-time between turning one FET off and the other on is not optional: overlap causes shoot-through (both FETs briefly conducting straight across the bus, a near dead-short). Too much dead-time instead forces the body diode of whichever FET is about to turn on to conduct first — its slow reverse-recovery (Q_rr) then causes extra loss when the other FET turns on. Tuning dead-time is a direct tradeoff between shoot-through risk and body-diode loss.
    Paralleling MOSFETs for current
    Because R_DS(on) has a positive temperature coefficient, MOSFETs naturally current-share when paralleled — a device that starts pulling more current heats up, its R_DS(on) rises, and current backs off toward the other devices. The only extra requirement is a small individual gate resistor (a few ohms) per device right at each gate, to damp any high-frequency oscillation between the parallel gates' input capacitances and driver output inductance — this is not optional at high di/dt even though DC sharing is inherently safe. BJTs need the opposite treatment: emitter ballast resistors, because BJT current gain increases with temperature, which would otherwise runaway hot devices into pulling all the current.

    10Component Selection Guide

    Parameters to check, in the order that actually matters, before you drop a part on a BOM

    MOSFET SELECTION CHECKLIST

    • V_DS(max)Rate for at least 1.3–1.5× the highest transient the drain will see (bus voltage + inductive ringing/leakage-inductance spikes), not just steady-state V_BUS.
    • I_D continuous / pulsedCheck both the continuous rating (thermally limited, package-dependent) and the pulsed rating (bonding-wire/SOA limited) against your actual duty cycle.
    • R_DS(on)Read it at the temperature and V_GS your driver will actually deliver — the datasheet headline number is usually at 25 °C and full V_GS, both optimistic.
    • V_GS(th) and its spreadThreshold varies unit-to-unit and drops with temperature. For logic-level drive (3.3/5 V), confirm the part is specified "logic level," not just "low R_DS(on) at 10 V."
    • Q_G (total gate charge)Directly sets driver current requirement and switching speed — smaller Q_G switches faster for the same driver, but often trades off against R_DS(on) (figure of merit R_DS(on)×Q_G is a useful single number to compare parts).
    • SOA (Safe Operating Area)Especially for linear-region use (hot-swap, linear regulators, motor braking) — a part can be well within V_DS(max) and I_D(max) individually and still be outside SOA when both are high simultaneously.
    • Avalanche energy (E_AS)Matters when the design relies on the body diode/avalanche clamp to survive inductive turn-off spikes rather than an external snubber/TVS.
    • Body diode Q_rr / t_rrCritical in any hard-switched half-bridge/synchronous topology; a slow body diode causes shoot-through-like loss even with correct dead-time.
    • Thermal resistance R_θJC / R_θJACombine with your loss budget (conduction + switching) to confirm junction temperature stays well below T_j(max) with real-world margin — not right at the limit.
    • Package & creepage/clearanceTO-220 vs SOT-23 vs DPAK vs LGA sets both thermal capability and PCB isolation distance for high-voltage designs.
    • Cost & second-source availabilityEspecially relevant for GaN/SiC parts, which still have fewer suppliers than mainstream Si.

    BJT SELECTION CHECKLIST

    • V_CEO / V_CBOBreakdown ratings, with margin for any inductive-load turn-off spike.
    • I_C(max), h_FE rangeDesign the base drive against the datasheet's minimum h_FE at your target I_C and temperature, never the typical/nominal value.
    • V_CE(sat) at your I_CSets conduction loss the same way R_DS(on) does for a MOSFET — check it's specified at a base drive ratio (I_C/I_B) you'll actually provide.
    • f_T (transition frequency)Sanity-check against your switching speed requirement; BJTs fall off fast above ~100 kHz due to minority-carrier storage time.
    • SOA and second breakdownBJTs have an extra failure mode (thermal second breakdown) inside their SOA that MOSFETs don't — a real constraint for linear-mode or hot-switching use.

    IGBT SELECTION CHECKLIST

    • V_CE(sat) and tail currentTail current at turn-off is IGBT-specific loss with no MOSFET equivalent — check E_off in the datasheet at your actual current/voltage/temperature, not just V_CE(sat).
    • Short-circuit withstand timeMotor-drive and inverter designs often require a rated µs-level short-circuit survival window for the protection loop to react.
    • Co-pack diode ratingMost IGBTs ship with (or need) an ultrafast antiparallel diode for freewheeling — check its Q_rr matches your switching frequency target.
