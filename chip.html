U1 — the chip itself

What the KSZ8895 actually does

It's a Layer 2 managed Ethernet switch on a single die: not a dumb repeater, but a chip that looks at each incoming packet's destination address and intelligently forwards it only to the port that needs it. Internally it contains:

  • 5 MACs (Media Access Controllers) — digital logic that handles Ethernet framing, addressing, and collision/flow handling.
  • 5 PHYs (Physical layer transceivers) — analog front-ends that drive and receive the actual electrical signals on copper cable.
  • A non-blocking switch fabric with a 1K-entry MAC address table, deciding packet-by-packet which port(s) to forward to.

4 of the 5 ports are typically normal copper RJ45 Ethernet ports, ready to plug a cable into. Port 5 is different — instead of a cable, it can connect chip-to-chip to a host processor, FPGA, or SoC via MII or RMII, so that host can inject and receive packets, and configure the switch's internal registers.

Managed vs. unmanaged "Managed" means the switch exposes configuration and monitoring — VLANs, port mirroring, rate limiting, spanning tree — over SPI, I2C, or MDC/MDIO. It can also be run in a self-contained "unmanaged" mode using strap pins or an EEPROM, with no host CPU needed at all.
REF — terminology

Terms you'll keep running into

MAC

Digital logic that frames Ethernet packets, handles addressing and collisions. The "brain" of a port.

PHY

Analog transceiver that converts digital bits to electrical signals on the wire, and back. The "muscle" of a port.

MII

Media Independent Interface — a parallel 4-bit-wide bus (25 MHz clock, TXD[3:0], RXD[3:0]...) connecting a MAC to a PHY, or here, Port 5 to a host.

RMII

Reduced MII — a slimmer 2-bit version needing a 50 MHz reference clock; fewer pins, common on microcontrollers.

Strap pin

A pin sampled at reset to set a default config (clock mode, interface mode). Often reused as an LED or signal pin afterward.

MDC/MDIO, SPI, I2C

Three interchangeable serial buses a host can use to read/write the switch's config registers. You only wire up one.

Magnetics

The isolation transformer between PHY and RJ45 jack, required by the Ethernet spec for noise rejection and galvanic isolation.

EEPROM (optional)

Lets the switch auto-load custom settings at boot with no host CPU present — for a standalone, pre-configured unmanaged switch.

How the support circuitry wraps around it

Four concerns surround the chip: power, timing, reset, and signal I/O. Click each block below for the detail.

B1 — power supply

Power rails and decoupling

The chip needs multiple voltage rails, not just a single 3.3V supply:

  • VDDIO (3.3V) — the I/O ring voltage for logic pins talking to the outside world.
  • Analog PHY supply (3.3V) — nominally the same rail, but separately filtered since the analog transceivers are noise-sensitive.
  • Core digital logic (1.8V or 1.2V) — generated by a separate on-board LDO regulator for internal switching logic.
Why separate the analog and digital rails?
Digital switching noise on the core logic supply can couple into the analog PHY circuitry and degrade signal integrity — more bit errors, worse return loss — if the two share a noisy rail without isolation. Designs typically use ferrite beads and independent decoupling networks to keep the analog supply quiet, even when it's nominally the same voltage as the digital one.
Why so many 0.1µF caps everywhere?
Every VDD pin needs a local decoupling capacitor right at the pin, because a 128-pin QFP has many power/ground pairs distributed around the die for low-inductance current delivery. Local caps supply the instantaneous current that PCB traces and planes can't deliver fast enough on their own — without them, the supply can sag ("droop") during simultaneous port switching, causing logic glitches or PHY jitter.

Y1 — reference clock

25 MHz crystal or oscillator

All internal timing — 125, 83, 41, 25, and 10 MHz clocks used across the switch fabric — is derived from one external 25 MHz reference on the X1/X2 pins.

  • Crystal option: a 25 MHz crystal plus two load capacitors (commonly ~15–22pF, matched to the crystal's spec) across X1/X2.
  • Oscillator option: a 3.3V-tolerant active 25 MHz oscillator module drives X1 directly; X2 is left open.
Why is 25MHz accuracy/jitter important?
100BASE-TX timing (125 Mbaud after 4B/5B encoding) and every internal switch-fabric clock are derived from this single reference through internal PLLs. Clock jitter directly affects bit error rate on the physical link, which is why datasheets specify tight ppm tolerances for the crystal — a sloppy clock source degrades the whole switch's link quality, not just one port.

If Port 5 is used in RMII mode, a 50MHz reference clock is also needed — either derived internally from the 25MHz crystal ("clock mode") or supplied externally ("normal mode"), selected via a strap pin.

R1/C1 — reset network

Power-on reset circuit

RST_N is active-low. The standard circuit is a simple RC power-on-reset: a resistor pulling RST_N high, with a capacitor to ground, so the pin stays low briefly after power-up while the cap charges, then releases.

RefComponentTypical valuePurpose

Why not tie RST_N straight to VDD?
Internal logic and PLLs need the supply rails to be stable before reset releases — otherwise the chip can boot into an undefined state. The RC delay guarantees reset is held long enough for power to settle before the chip starts running.
J1–J4 — line-side & management I/O

Magnetics, termination, and the management bus

Per copper port (×4)

  • Isolation transformer ("magnetics") — discrete or built into the RJ45 jack ("magjack"). Required for common-mode noise rejection and galvanic isolation between your board's ground and the far-end device, per the Ethernet spec.
  • Termination resistors — typically 49.9Ω per line on the differential TX/RX pairs, matching the cable/transformer impedance to minimize reflections.
  • Common-mode choke (optional) — extra EMI suppression, more common in industrial/automotive-adjacent designs (this family has AEC-Q100 variants).

Bias-setting resistor

A precision resistor from the ISET pin to ground — commonly 12.4kΩ, 1% — sets the PHY's internal analog bias current, calibrating transmit signal amplitude.

Why does 1% tolerance matter here?
This resistor directly calibrates the analog transmit driver current. Ethernet's TX amplitude and return-loss requirements are part of IEEE 802.3 conformance, so a loose resistor tolerance can push transmit levels out of spec — especially noticeable over longer cable runs.

Strap-in / configuration pins

Pins like the Port 5 mode select, management-interface select, and clock-mode select are sampled at reset. External pull-up or pull-down resistors (commonly ~10kΩ) override the chip's internal defaults when your application needs a non-default mode — e.g. Port 5 as RMII instead of MII.

Management interface

Pick one of MDC/MDIO, SPI, or I2C for host configuration. Open-drain signals on these buses (MDIO, SPI data, SDA, and the INTR_N interrupt output) need external pull-ups since open-drain outputs can only pull low.

NOTE — putting it together

The mental model

Four concerns wrap around one chip:

  1. Power — multiple clean, properly decoupled and sequenced voltage rails.
  2. Timing — one accurate, low-jitter 25MHz clock reference.
  3. Reset — a controlled power-on sequencing signal.
  4. Signal I/O — 4 copper ports with magnetics and termination, plus Port 5 wired to a host via MII/RMII, plus a management bus with pull-ups for configuration.
